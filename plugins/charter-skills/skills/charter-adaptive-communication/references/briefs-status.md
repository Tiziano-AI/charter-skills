# Briefs and status

For a status update, handoff, decision brief or summary whose receiver was
not in the work, once the work calls for one: what to select from the
established material and how to express it.

## Select by the receiver's next use

A brief is not a miniature transcript. Its job is to make the consequential
meaning recoverable without replaying discovery. A sponsor may need what is
usable, what remains unresolved and what decision is needed. A collaborator
continuing a specific part may need an exact object, the chosen approach and
the reason a tempting alternative was rejected. Those are different cuts
through the same established material.

> The bilingual form is ready for content review. The Italian labels are
> translated; the email confirmation is still English. Decide whether that
> remaining difference is acceptable for Friday's pilot.

This invented example gives a receiver the state and decision. “Translation
work progressed well, with several items completed and one open question”
sounds positive but makes the reader reconstruct both. An exhaustive list
of each label translated is not automatically better; its value depends on
whether this receiver needs to review those exact labels.

## A change needs its consequence, not its whole chronology

Status language often confuses activity with a changed result. “We reviewed
the layouts” describes work performed. “The mobile layout now keeps the
submit button visible” describes a different claim. Use whichever claim the
supplied evidence actually supports. Do not promote the first into the
second to create a stronger update.

A useful status passage can connect a meaningful change with its consequence,
present state and intended next action in ordinary prose. It need not use
four headings. “The supplier confirmed the revised dimensions, so the
cabinet can fit the alcove. The finish is still undecided; tomorrow's sample
review is intended to settle it” preserves completion and intention in one
account. “The cabinet is ready” would collapse separate states.

Repeated unchanged background can conceal the delta. But removing a still
relevant limitation merely because it is old can falsely imply that it has
been resolved. “The export now includes captions; editable charts are still
unsupported” preserves the live boundary without retelling its history.

Time references also depend on where the text will be read. “Tomorrow's
review” can be clear in today's exchange but ambiguous in a forwarded brief.
Where that ambiguity matters, use the established date: “the review on
15 October.” If the source does not establish a date, do not calculate one
from an assumed sending time. Distinguish an observation's time from the
event's time: “At 10:00, the portal showed the application as received” does
not say it arrived at 10:00. Include a time zone when it changes how the
receiver interprets a supplied deadline, without turning every casual reply
into a timestamped record.

## Context must survive a change of receiver

“Continue with the approved approach” is enough only if the recipient can
identify that approach and the scope of approval. A useful isolated passage
might instead say “Use the two-column layout for the workshop handout; the
single-column version was rejected because it split each exercise across
pages.” This preserves a decision and its reason without carrying a diary.
It must not imply approval for other artifacts or other effects.

Agent recipients especially benefit from exact identities where titles or
pronouns could refer to several objects. Human recipients may need a
descriptive label alongside that identity. The destination's own format
governs how these are expressed.

Useful repetition is selective. A short standalone summary may need the
product name even though it appears above in the original conversation.
A long brief may repeat the applicable period at a table whose data could be
read independently. Repeating the full premise at every paragraph can bury
the distinctions the recipient needs next.

## Compression should leave evidence and states recognizable

“All checks passed” is not faithful to “All three checks in the supplied
sample passed.” “Shipped” may mean dispatched, installed or available to
users; choose the actual state rather than the most celebratory shorthand.
“Non ho ancora ricevuto conferma” preserves uncertainty; “Non è arrivato”
turns missing confirmation into a claimed failure of arrival.

Where a source link, exact identifier or quotation is part of the supplied
meaning needed for follow-up, keep it reachable and attached to the claim it
supports. A summary label such as “technical issues” can erase distinctions
between an observed defect and an untested property. The text should preserve
that contrast without expanding every brief into an evidence archive.

Headings and tables should earn their space. A small comparison of current
and intended states can be useful; a generic red/amber/green table can imply
ratings that were never made. A plain paragraph may be the most legible form
for one material change. Briefness is a consequence of selection, not a
word-budget rule that licenses dropping meaning.
