# Instructions and consequential text

For procedures, requirements, forms, notices and other passages a reader may
act on: keeping the scope, conditions and obligations of the supplied
substance intact in the wording. The procedure, requirement or permission
itself comes from whoever owns it.

## Conditions belong at the point of action

A reader can begin acting before reaching the end of a paragraph. Put an
established prerequisite before the action that depends on it, especially
where skimming could make a later qualification disappear.

> After the preview shows the intended folder, choose **Move**. If it shows
> another folder, choose **Cancel**.

This presents two supplied branches. “Choose **Move**. Check that the folder
was right” is not a shorter version: it changes the order and may change the
effect. Conversely, adding a confirmation step simply because the text is
consequential invents procedure. Composition may expose a missing condition;
it cannot manufacture the condition's content or authority.

Keep each branch's consequence close enough to its condition to avoid
cross-reading. A shared instruction can follow several branches when it
really applies to all of them. Repeating it under each branch may imply
separate operations; placing a branch-specific instruction after the group
may falsely make it universal. Layout is part of scope, not decoration.

Consider this dense but fully specified illustrative instruction:

> If the preview shows the intended chart and its reporting period matches
> the report, copy the chart into the report, enter the reporting period in
> the caption, then save the report; otherwise close the preview and leave
> the report unchanged.

Its positive branch can become easier to use without detaching the
conditions or adding a step:

**If the preview shows the intended chart and its reporting period matches
the report:**

1. Copy the chart into the report.
2. Enter the reporting period in the caption.
3. Save the report.

**Otherwise:** close the preview and leave the report unchanged.

Both conditions still govern all three steps; “otherwise” still covers a
failure of either condition. Turning the conditions into a note below the
steps would change their practical scope for someone reading in order.
Splitting them into two independent branches could allow one condition to
stand in for the other. The paragraph version remains useful when readers
need an overview rather than a working procedure; a multi-step branch earns
its space when it makes the supplied sequence easier to follow.

## Required limits must not disappear into notes

Notes usually look supplementary. A condition that determines whether an
action is permitted or appropriate belongs with that action, not in a
footnote that looks optional. If the supplied instruction is “Publish only
the public version; internal comments appear in the published packet,”
“Publish the packet” followed by “Note: consider the comments” loses both
the restriction and its reason. “Publish only the public version” keeps the
required limit in the action; the explanation about comments can sit just
before it where it helps the reader understand the consequence.

A warning's location should let it inform the affected action before the
reader takes it. Its wording should carry the established hazard or
consequence and relevant restriction, rather than a generic “Be careful.”
This does not authorize inventing a hazard, severity label or safety step.
When a domain has a prescribed warning form, preserve that form; composition
does not substitute its own convention. Nor should every helpful aside be
promoted into a warning: that can make the genuine limitation harder to
recognize.

## Obligation, permission and expectation are different

“Must,” “may,” “should” and “will” are not stylistic substitutes. A requirement
does not become a suggestion to sound friendly, and a likely result does not
become a promise to sound decisive. Preserve defined normative language in
its own context. There is no need to impose a new keyword system on a text
whose owner uses ordinary language.

> Submit the form by 18:00 on 14 October to be included in this review.

If that is the established condition, “Please send it soon” loses the
deadline and consequence. “We guarantee review of every form received by
18:00” adds a promise. In Italian, “Puoi allegare una foto” permits an
attachment; “Devi allegare una foto” requires it; “La foto sarà allegata”
describes a future state. Smoother wording cannot justify moving among them.

An imperative should distinguish the action's object and target when they
matter: “Copy the reference number into **Payment reference**” is clearer
than “Add it below.” The shorter version can still fit a UI where the number
and field are immediately visible and unambiguous. Context determines the
needed explicitness, not a blanket demand to repeat every noun.

## Actions are not their outcomes

Procedural and descriptive passages have different jobs. “When the reviewer
selects **Approve**, the form records the review date” describes a system
relationship. “Select **Approve** to record the review date” instructs the
reader to act and supplies a purpose for that action. The latter is not a
stylistic improvement if the text was explaining behavior rather than asking
for approval. Conversely, a procedure expressed entirely as descriptions
can leave the reader unsure which actions are theirs. Preserve who is being
asked to do what, and keep contextual explanations distinguishable from
commands without forcing them into separate documents.

“Choose **Send**” is an action. “The recipient receives the message” is an
outcome. A procedure can separately state the expected feedback: “A
submission number appears.” Do not turn that expectation into a claim that
it happened or that delivery is complete. Similarly, a notice saying
“Your request was received” should not be edited to “Your request was
approved” merely because the latter sounds more reassuring.

For an error, start with the known failure and the supplied available action.
“The upload stopped before confirmation. The outcome is unknown” is useful
when that is all the evidence supports. “Your connection failed; try again”
adds both a diagnosis and a recovery instruction. Neither belongs to the
writer's stylistic discretion. A precise account of an unknown can be more
actionable than a confident invented explanation.

## Exact strings coexist with explanatory prose

Field names, commands, identifiers, quoted clauses and machine-readable values
may have byte-sensitive consumers. Explain them around the literal rather
than translating or tidying the literal itself. A paragraph may say “Choose
Italian,” while the receiving field requires `it-IT`. Those are different
surfaces of one instruction, not synonyms freely interchangeable everywhere.

In a long requirement, bullets can separate independent obligations, while a
sentence can keep a tightly scoped exception together. A table is useful for
comparable permissions; it is dangerous when a blank cell could mean either
“not permitted” or “not specified.” State the intended meaning of absence if
the source supplies it. Do not silently fill an unknown to make the table neat.

The opposite of ambiguity is not legalistic bulk. Repeated warnings can hide
the one condition relevant at a particular action, and excessive formality
can make a simple request sound compulsory. Match precision to the actual
content while preserving every boundary the source establishes.
