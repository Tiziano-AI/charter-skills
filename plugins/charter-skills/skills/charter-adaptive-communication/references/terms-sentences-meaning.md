# Terms, sentences and meaning

For a passage that is hard to interpret because its names, attachments or
logical structure are unclear: sentence-level craft that works within each
domain's own vocabulary. Its examples are invented contrasts.

## Identity is often more important than variation

Elegant variation can be expensive where the receiver is tracking objects.
“The application sends a request. The task waits in a queue. The operation
expires after ten minutes” leaves open whether the same object changes name
or three objects have different lifecycles. If one request is meant, repeating
“request” removes a false distinction. If three objects are meant, name the
relationship: “The request creates a task; the task's execution is an
operation.” The explanation must come from the actual domain, not from the
editor's wish to reconcile the words.

This is local identity, not a ban on synonyms. “The room was narrow, almost a
corridor” uses a second noun to alter the image rather than to label another
room. A text may also quote someone else's term before distinguishing it from
its own. Technical consistency protects reference; it need not erase voice.

An unfamiliar term earns an explanation at the point where it matters.
“A checksum, a short value calculated from the file's contents, lets us
compare the copies” supplies the relationship needed for this sentence.
It does not teach the whole subject or claim that every matching checksum
proves identity under every method. For readers already using checksums,
the gloss adds friction. For a nontechnical reader who only needs the result,
“The copies match under this check” may fit better if that preserves the
established claim. Simplification changes the presentation, not the proof.

## Syntax should expose attachment and scope

A sentence's difficulty often comes from having to hold unresolved parts,
not from its word count. “The revised request that the manager submitted
after the team reviewed the application was rejected” nests the review and
submission inside the subject of the rejection. “The manager submitted the
revised request after the team reviewed the application. That request was
rejected” exposes the same actors, actions and temporal relation without
claiming who revised or rejected it. The second version helps when the
receiver needs to follow the sequence; the first can still fit a passage
focused on which request was rejected. Splitting is not inherently a repair.

Noun groups compress relationships by leaving their links implicit.
“Customer account access review records” requires the reader to infer which
words belong together. If the intended meaning is records of reviews of
access to customer accounts, “records of access reviews for customer
accounts” makes the grouping easier to recover. If the records instead
describe customers' reviews of an account-access feature, that rewrite is
wrong. An editor cannot choose the grouping from fluency alone.

Keep established compound terms together: expanding “heat pump” into “pump
for heat” does not improve a technical passage. Where a long group has a
stable domain meaning, name it once with the needed explanation, then use
that name. Where relations remain hard to see, prepositions or a clause can
expose them. Hyphens can show a local modifier grouping, but they cannot
repair an unknown relationship or justify an arbitrary cap on noun groups.

Conditions and exceptions deserve particular care. Compare:

> You may reuse the diagram in internal training if you retain its credit,
> but not in external advertising.

with:

> You may reuse the diagram. Retain its credit. Do not advertise externally.

The second version loses the internal-training restriction and changes a
restriction on diagram use into a wider instruction about advertising. Keep
the first sentence, or give the permitted and excluded uses separate explicit
branches. Similarly, “Only the editor may change the title” limits the actor;
“The editor may change only the title” limits the object. Moving “only” is a
substantive edit.

Negation, modals and quantifiers also change meaning through their scope.
“Not all files were copied” leaves open which files and how many; “No files
were copied” asserts zero. “You do not have to replace both parts” removes
an obligation to replace both; it does not mean “You must not replace both
parts,” which forbids doing so. “The service may not start” can describe a
possibility or a prohibition. If the intended meaning is uncertainty, “The
service might fail to start” distinguishes it from “Starting the service is
not permitted.” That choice requires the actual meaning, not a preference
for one modal verb.

Quantifier relationships can be obscure even without negation. “Each group
uses one room” permits a different room for each group; “All groups use the
same room” does not. “Check a sample from every batch” is not equivalent to
“Check every sample from a batch.” In Italian, “Non tutti i controlli sono
conclusi” leaves some completion possible; “Nessun controllo è concluso”
excludes it. Make the relation explicit when it matters, without padding
every unambiguous everyday sentence with formal logic.

Pronouns reduce needless repetition when the antecedent is unambiguous.
“Sara sent Elena her notes” is not reliable when ownership matters. “Sara sent
her own notes to Elena” or “Sara sent Elena's notes back to her” resolves
different meanings; the editor must not choose one without the supplied
meaning. In Italian, “Il responsabile ha avvisato il tecnico quando era
pronto” has the same problem: specify who or what was ready if known.

## Put the useful action back into the sentence

Nominalizations can hide actors, actions and timing: “Implementation of the
revision is subject to completion of approval” may become “Implement the
revision after the editor approves it” only if that actor and dependency are
established. If the process is known but the approver is not, “Implement the
revision after it is approved” is appropriately passive. Naming an actor
without support is not a clarity improvement.

Some nouns are the topic, not clutter. “Approval takes two days” is a useful
statement about a process. Recasting it as “Someone approves it in two days”
adds an irrelevant person and may change elapsed time into a promise about
an individual's action. Choose the grammatical subject that sustains the
passage's focus without obscuring a relevant responsibility.

## Compression has a semantic cost

An edit can remove words while multiplying possible interpretations.
“Revenue rose 8% in the pilot stores during June” cannot safely become
“Revenue rose 8%”: scope and period disappear. Nor can “The result is
consistent with wear, but does not establish it” become “Wear is the cause.”

Qualify the affected claim rather than smearing uncertainty over the whole
passage. “The connector fits. Its outdoor lifetime is not yet known” preserves
a known property and an unknown one. “The connector may possibly work” makes
both vague. Conversely, a tightly qualified claim must not acquire confidence
just because its caveat interrupts the rhythm. The aim is an easily recovered
meaning, not the shortest grammatical string.

## Worked contrasts

- Concrete verbs reveal what stacked nouns conceal: "The editor approves the
  draft" reads more easily than "draft approval execution by editorial."
- Active voice is not worth inventing an actor: "The file was removed; the log
  does not identify who removed it" is more faithful than an unsupported "The
  administrator removed the file."
- Repeat "the reviewer" when "she" could mean either of two people.
- Explain a term where the receiver meets it: "The deductible, the amount you
  pay before this coverage pays, ..." serves better than unexplained jargon or
  a vague substitute such as "the fee."
- Deliberate wordplay, quotation and meaningful distinctions are not
  terminology errors. There is no global approved vocabulary.
