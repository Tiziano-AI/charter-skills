# Recurring first-draft forms

Forms that turn up in drafts whatever their subject or occasion, as described
in November 2026; they shift with the model, its instructions and the year.
Open this when drafts on different subjects keep taking the same shape. Each
form has a use, given beside it. The examples are invented.

## A report frame in place of an account

A bold label for every sentence, balanced sections and a closing takeaway can
give a small change the shape of a report before there is anything to navigate.
“Summary: search improved. Impact: better discovery. Next steps: more coverage”
leaves the reader to find the change inside the headings. “The index now
includes pen names; searching either name reaches the same entry. Birth dates
are still missing” gives this update its own content and ends at the boundary.

A long report with distinct findings needs navigation. A short comparison may
need a table because each reader wants to locate the same attribute. Keep that
structure when it serves a real reading task; fewer headings are not the goal.

## Importance in place of the thing that matters

“An important step toward making the archive easier to use” announces a value
judgment; “On Thursdays, the archive now stays open until 20:00” gives the reader
the change. Repeated claims of significance, abstract benefits and tails such
as “highlighting its importance” can occupy the place where an explanation
belongs. Replacing one fashionable verb with another leaves that gap intact.

An argument can need a statement of significance, and “importantly” can mark a
real difference in weight. Give the reason or fact it points to. A participial
clause can express an actual simultaneous action or consequence; its grammar
is not the defect. Cut the interpretation only when it adds nothing, and keep
a causal relation only at the strength the account supports.

## A social wrapper, or clipped labels, in place of a relationship

General praise and sincerity intensifiers can make a thank-you sound warm
without revealing what the sender values. The opposite default turns a human
message into “Cause: cache. Action: rebuild.” Find the actual gesture, concern
or connection: the glasses a friend washed, the incompatible export, the
reason one action follows another. Use sentences when the reader needs that
connection, and a field label when someone is scanning a diagnostic record.

A conventional greeting can be the right social opening. A plain acknowledgment
can be complete. Neither needs an unusual detail to pass a test of originality;
the question is whether the wording performs this message's intended act.

## A dramatic shape in place of movement

Successive “not X, but Y” turns, balanced threes and one-line revelations can
give unrelated points the same theatrical weight. The reader keeps meeting a
new climax rather than following a thought. Let the strongest finding or image
carry the emphasis, and let the other sentences connect and develop it.

Use a contrast when X is a misconception the reader actually holds, parallel
phrasing for genuinely parallel ideas, and repetition when its return changes
the experience. A speech may build through several such returns. A working
answer may have no rhetorical turn at all. Changing punctuation or avoiding
every triad would merely substitute another uniform style.

## A borrowed figure in place of the subject's own

Some figures arrive whatever the subject: a kitchen or a library to explain
any system, a dial worth turning for any option, a journey for any change, a
promise to “unlock” or “elevate” in any promotion, air “thick with” something
in any tense scene. They make a passage sound written while telling the
reader nothing about this case. Look for the comparison in the reader's own
work or the subject's material: a stage manager may grasp two processes
overwriting each other as two crew members resetting the same prop between
scenes, each undoing the other's placement. Where nothing in the material
does that work, the literal sentence is the better one. A genre's stock can
be the point in a pastiche, a parody or a form whose readers expect it.

## Evidence

Two studies measured some of these patterns in the models and English corpora
they tested: noun-heavy writing and participial clauses
([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)) and model-specific
formatting ([Sun et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12150v2)). The other
forms here are editorial description.
