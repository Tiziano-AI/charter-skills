# Charter · Research & Decisions

“Does the venue's quote include the piano?” and “Which venue should we hire?”
may depend on the same document. For the first, find the terms that apply to
this room and date. A venue can own a piano without including its use in the
hire price. If the quote settles it, give the answer with the clause and
stop. For the second, that clause changes the cost of an alternative; it
doesn't establish what a better instrument is worth to the performers.

## What can a source settle?

State the claim precisely enough that the wrong subject, version, region or
date would change the answer. Before weighing conflicting reports or surveying
a field, read [Current fact research](current-fact-research.md) to choose
evidence suited to the claim. The organizer's rules define who is eligible;
someone turned away at the door has evidence about how the rule was applied
on that occasion. Neither source establishes both facts. An announcement's
publication date need not be the date its terms take effect, and ten articles
tracing back to that announcement are still one origin.

For [claims that still conflict](references/workflow.md), align definitions,
scope and effective dates before treating the disagreement as substantive.
For a review, look for evidence the first search would miss, including studies
that used a different term for the same outcome. Say which population or part
of the field the evidence covers. Give the supported answer, its direct
source and any unresolved condition that changes its use; don't manufacture
a choice just to recommend something.

## What would make another option preferable?

Before setting criteria for a consequential choice, read
[Decision design](decision-design.md). Start from what participants have
already supplied: a maximum price removes bids above it, a preferred price
does not, and an offer to help is not an accepted staffing commitment. If
one feasible option is at least as good on every material concern and better
on one, explain why it wins. Otherwise identify the benefit worth giving
something up for and who bears the loss. Read [framing and alternatives](references/decision-workflow.md)
when delay or a staged commitment changes the comparison. Use the
[shared-decision method](references/contributions-and-decision-rights.md)
when agreement on the facts still leaves several people's commitments open.

A switching point can be more useful than a forecast. Suppose two print
bids for the same edition are 200 plus 3 per copy, or 5 per copy with no
setup fee, in the same currency. They tie at 100 copies. At 101 the first
costs 503 and the second 505; at 99 the second saves 2. If every credible
order is below 100, a more precise demand estimate won't change which costs
less. If unsold copies or cash paid up front matter, price alone no longer
settles the choice. Read [quantitative methods](references/quantitative-methods.md)
before assigning weights or probabilities, and [commitment and learning](references/reversibility-and-learning.md)
before recommending a pilot whose cost or exit terms could defeat its purpose.

Give the choice, the downside it accepts and the change that would make you
choose differently. A recommendation does not place the order. Where the
alternatives are ways to win a market, take the customer, current workaround
and competing offers to `charter-product-strategy-and-design`; compare the
bets on switching, distribution and economics. Where the decisive premise
is which AI produces more usable results, give
`charter-ai-evaluation-and-session-learning` the actual task and costly
failure, and use observed performance rather than vendor claims.
