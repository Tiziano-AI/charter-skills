# Adaptive interviewing examples

Use these cases when the next question is hard to choose, an analogy has more
than one consequential reading, or exploration risks changing the commission.
They deepen Work Discovery's method; they are not an intake sequence.

## A concrete episode changes the next question

"Walk me through the last time this became difficult" can expose a workaround,
an interruption or a dependency that "What features do you want?" never
reaches. If the person loses their place when interrupted, ask what lets them
resume rather than continuing through a prepared feature list. An account of
what happened and a wish for what should happen inform each other; neither
replaces the other.

An episode is useful because it connects a difficulty to circumstances and
consequences, not because every person must supply a story. If an artifact or
existing record already reveals the mechanism, inspect it instead of making
the person reconstruct the event for an interview.

## An analogy can conceal different needs

"Like a handwritten notebook" might concern intimacy, free layout or
handwriting itself. Those readings can imply different requirements. A neutral
contrast exposes them without promoting one interpretation into the brief:
"Would a typed page with free placement still feel like a notebook, or is
handwriting what matters?"

The answer might reveal privacy or a place to keep unfinished thoughts instead.
Follow that concern; the original options were aids to understanding, not an
exhaustive ballot. The person can combine them or reject the framing. Compare
the consequence of losing a property before treating it as necessary: free
placement may be negotiable, while keeping private notes off a shared screen
may not be.

## One subject can imply different commissioned work

A cake request might mean a recipe, a diagnosis of a failed bake or practical
help making one. A proposed product can similarly be the subject of a critique,
a market decision, a specification or implementation. The subject tells you
where to look; the intended result tells you what to do and when it is useful.

When the requested result is a specification, research, functional and
experiential design, architecture and technical choices can be substantive
parts of producing it. A bounded feasibility trial may resolve a premise the
specification depends on. That trial does not turn the commission into a
production implementation. Conversely, a request for a repair or an image
cannot be completed merely by describing how someone might make it.

An architectural approval settles the architecture actually presented, not
every remaining functional choice or authority to deploy. Carry the settled
part forward and explore only the uncertainty that changes the next action.

## Basis and limits

The method draws on
[GOV.UK's in-depth interviewing guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-in-depth-interviews)
for neutral questions, concrete experiences and adaptive follow-up, and
[NASA's stakeholder expectations guidance](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/4-1-stakeholder-expectations-definition/)
for distinguishing desired results from implementation and agreeing their
meaning. These sources concern service research and engineering respectively;
their formal processes are not required for this collaboration.
