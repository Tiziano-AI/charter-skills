# Taxonomy Selection

Choose the primary axis from the archive owner's most common retrieval question and the collection's
lifecycle. Use secondary axes below it or as metadata.

## Candidate axes

### Subject or domain

Examples: finance, legal, housing, health, clients, research, media. This works when users usually
start with “what kind of matter is this?” and subjects remain stable over time.

Risk: broad subjects become dumping grounds, and one item may legitimately span several domains.

### Entity or relationship

Examples: client, project, property, person, account, case, product. This works when users usually
start with “who or what is this about?” and each entity has a meaningful internal lifecycle.

Risk: shared material is duplicated across entities or hidden in an arbitrary one.

### Lifecycle

Examples: intake, active, delivered, reference, archived. This works for processing roots and
delivery flows where the next action matters more than long-term subject browsing.

Risk: completed material loses subject context, and items stall in vague active or archive buckets.

### Time

Examples: year, quarter, event date, tax period. This works when chronology is the dominant lookup
and dates are trustworthy.

Risk: undated or multi-period records become hard to place, and copy timestamps masquerade as event
dates.

### Source or provenance

Examples: device, camera, application, mailbox, data export, collecting institution. This works for
forensic, research, and preservation archives where origin is more important than topic.

Risk: everyday retrieval by subject becomes slow, and source changes fragment one continuing body of
work.

### Geography

Examples: country, site, property, region. This works when location truly owns the material and
cross-location work is uncommon.

Risk: jurisdiction, residence, event location, and storage location are conflated.

## Selection exercise

1. Use retrieval prompts from real use, including the common case and consequential exceptions.
2. Test each candidate axis against them: how many folder decisions are needed before the target
   becomes visible?
3. Place several ambiguous, cross-cutting, and recurring items into the candidate structure.
4. Check whether new material has an obvious intake and promotion path.
5. Choose one dominant top-level axis; use names, metadata, indexes, or lower levels for the others.
6. Reconsider physical reorganization if search or a generated index answers the prompts with less
   disruption.

## Hybrid taxonomies

A hybrid is justified when the root contains genuinely distinct collection states, such as an intake
zone plus a stable client archive, or originals plus derivatives in a media collection. It is not
justified because two competing top-level schemes are both familiar.

Keep each level internally consistent. Mixing `clients`, `2025`, `downloads`, and `signed` as peers
forces the user to remember historical placement decisions rather than reason from the taxonomy.
