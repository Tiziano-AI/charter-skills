# Archive Model

An archive is successful when a future user can retrieve, interpret, and trust its material without
reconstructing the cleanup project. Visual neatness is secondary.

## Retrieval questions

Before choosing folders, collect a few real retrieval prompts:

- “Find the signed agreement for client A.”
- “Show the source and final exports for project B.”
- “Find tax records for year C in jurisdiction D.”
- “Recover the original photos from event E.”
- “Which version was delivered, and what preceded it?”

The nouns and qualifiers in these prompts reveal the primary axis, useful metadata, necessary
chronology, and version relationships. If search or an index answers them better than physical
moves, reorganize less.

## Archive states

Do not force unlike roots into one taxonomy:

- **Intake:** newly arrived material awaits classification or processing.
- **Working collection:** active files change frequently and may be owned by applications.
- **Reference collection:** stable material is retrieved repeatedly but rarely edited.
- **Record archive:** preservation, chronology, provenance, and retention dominate convenience.
- **Media library:** originals, edits, proxies, and exports have explicit derivative relationships.
- **Delivery archive:** client, engagement, milestone, and delivered-version identity dominate.
- **Backup or replica:** location redundancy is intentional and should not be “deduplicated” as a
  content collection.

A root may contain lifecycle zones, but each zone should have a clear purpose and transition.

## Canonical placement and alternate access

Give each item or bundle one authoritative home inside the collection. Serve secondary lookup needs
with search, tags, metadata, saved queries, indexes, or deliberate aliases rather than copied folder
trees. Two editable “canonical” copies invite divergence.

Some items are inseparable bundles: a project file plus linked media, a website export with assets,
a mail archive plus indexes, a photo RAW with sidecar, or a software-generated library. Preserve
bundle boundaries unless the owning application explicitly supports relocation.

## Provenance and chronology

Paths, source directories, timestamps, file-system metadata, embedded metadata, manifests, naming
conventions, and neighboring files can all carry provenance. Moving or renaming may improve
retrieval while weakening that history.

Preserve original names or paths in a mapping when they carry identifiers, acquisition order, legal
context, or reconstruction value. Treat modification time cautiously: copy, download, sync, and
conversion operations often rewrite it. Prefer embedded capture or creation dates and source records
when chronology matters.

## Versions and derivatives

Model relationships instead of flattening them:

- **version:** a later editable state of the same work;
- **derivative:** export, crop, transcode, preview, print file, or flattened copy;
- **component:** a file required by a larger bundle;
- **duplicate:** byte-identical content appearing in more than one path;
- **replica:** an intentionally redundant copy for backup or distribution;
- **variant:** a parallel language, format, audience, or creative treatment.

Names such as `final`, `final2`, and `latest` do not establish these relationships. Use source
context, application metadata, embedded timestamps, or human classification.

## Ambiguity as a maintained state

Unclear material needs a visible review path, not a plausible guess. Keep its original identity,
record the competing interpretations, and assign a revisit trigger. Quarantine should remain small,
searchable, and actively classifiable; a giant `misc` folder merely hides taxonomy failure.

## Consequences of movement

Before moving a collection, identify dependencies outside the tree: application libraries, relative
links, aliases, sync clients, backup selection, shared links, permissions, file IDs, automation,
retention rules, and human muscle memory. The right intervention may be a new index, naming repair,
or intake rule rather than a complete move.
