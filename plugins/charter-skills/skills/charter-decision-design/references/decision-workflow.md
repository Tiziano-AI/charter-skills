# Framing the actual decision

A decision frame defines which consequences count. It can change the preferred
option before any arithmetic begins. Choosing a tool for a bounded delivery,
choosing a continuing platform, and deciding whether to migrate an existing
system are different choices even when the product names are identical.

## Scope, horizon and constraints

The decision owner supplies the objective and legitimate trade-offs. Other
stakeholders supply important consequences and constraints but need not have
the same preferences. Blending those preferences into an unexplained average
can erase the actual conflict.

The horizon determines which costs and benefits belong. An expensive migration
can dominate long-run operating savings at a short horizon; a deadline can make
delay a real loss rather than a neutral option. Sunk costs that cannot change
are not future costs, while future switching costs created by today's choice
are relevant.

A requirement that truly eliminates an option is a constraint, not a large
negative weight. If every option fails, that reveals a feasibility problem:
the choice may require changing scope or a person-owned requirement rather
than declaring the least infeasible score the winner.

## Alternatives are causal paths

Useful options connect action to outcome through a plausible mechanism.
Variants with the same decisive trade-off can be grouped; a staged choice
deserves its own option when it changes exposure or information.

Status quo has consequences too: maintenance, decay, foregone opportunities
or contract expiry. Delay differs from status quo when information arrives,
options disappear, or commitments become harder. Including either as a token
row creates a biased comparison.

An apparent choice between two products can conceal a more useful choice
between operating models. But expanding the frame is justified only when that
alternative is feasible for the user's actual decision, not because it is
interesting in principle.

## Inputs carry different kinds of uncertainty

An observed fact can be wrong or out of date. An estimate has a range. An
assumption closes a gap provisionally. An inference depends on its premises.
A preference is not an empirical claim to be researched away.

These distinctions determine the response to disagreement. Competing factual
claims call for better evidence or uncertainty modeling; competing preferences
call for making the trade-off explicit to its owner. Averaging the two layers
does not reconcile them.

Criteria need distinct concerns and interpretable measures. A simple working
representation can hold criterion, scale, direction, threshold or weight, and
evidence. It is useful only to the extent that it exposes a trade-off. Duplicate
criteria count one benefit repeatedly, while an option-specific criterion can
smuggle in the intended winner.

## A recommendation explains its stability

A decision-ready answer leads with the choice and the causal reason it wins.
The strongest counterargument identifies where the recommendation is weakest.
Sensitivity makes that weakness usable by locating the premise at which another
option becomes preferable.

Implementation consequences matter when they change the choice: required
migration, staffing, irreversible exposure or timing. A revisit trigger is an
observable change in a decisive premise, not a requirement to reopen the
decision on a calendar. Enough model should remain for the owner to recompute
the choice when that premise actually changes.
