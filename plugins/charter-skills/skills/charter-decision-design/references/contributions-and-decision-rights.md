# Contributions, disagreement and decision rights

Use this when a choice depends on several people's knowledge, preferences or
commitments. A decision can be analytically sound yet unusable because it treats
a proposal as consent, averages away a genuine conflict or asks the wrong person
to accept its consequences.

## Read contributions as answers to different questions

A participant may supply an observation, a diagnosis, a proposed action, a
constraint or an offer. These statements have different force. "Our current
system cannot do this" is evidence to inspect; "we will not maintain another
system" states a preference or commitment boundary; "we could host it" is an
option, not an accepted operational responsibility.

Preserve the conditions under which a contribution was made. A partner who
accepts a prototype using synthetic data has not accepted production custody of
customer records. A correction to one account's use does not retire a second
account with a separate purpose. Similar nouns can hide materially different
objects; compare scope and consequences before calling the statements
contradictory.

## Disagreement has a mechanism

| Difference | What can resolve it |
| --- | --- |
| Different observations of the same object | Exact source, version, scope or a discriminating observation |
| Same facts, different causal expectations | Explicit mechanisms, uncertainty ranges or a bounded trial that can distinguish them |
| Same consequences, different preferences | The trade-off stated to its decision owner |
| Different definitions of success | Agreement on the outcome being commissioned |
| Conflicting claims about who must act | Existing agreements and the person who controls that commitment |
| Different options assumed feasible | Inspect the actual capability or constraint before scoring |

These layers can coexist. A disagreement about architecture may contain both an
empirical dispute about capability and a legitimate unwillingness to own its
operations. More technical evidence can settle the first without settling the
second. Consensus language that hides the latter creates a commitment failure.

## Locate who can accept each consequence

Map rights only as far as the live choice requires. Who can select the option?
Whose resources or obligations does it commit? Who supplies expertise without
holding a veto? The loudest contributor, most senior title and best-informed
expert need not be the same as the authorized decision owner.

Shared decisions may require several permissions rather than a single winner.
For example, a supplier can choose its internal implementation while a customer
chooses whether to send regulated data. Frame the recommendation so each party
can accept its own consequence; do not use one party's approval as a substitute
for another's.

## Ask only what separates the remaining options

When a participant's answer is necessary, ask the smallest question that
distinguishes the remaining alternatives rather than presenting every
historical branch again. State what the supplied material already settles, so
the answer can be applied without reopening it; a full questionnaire sent back
after a concrete proposal discards that work and hides the real uncertainty.
