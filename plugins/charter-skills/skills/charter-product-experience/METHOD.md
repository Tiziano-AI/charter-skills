# Charter · Product Experience

An experience is the relationship between the result people seek, the work a
product asks of them, and what they learn to expect from it. A beautiful screen
can sit inside a bad experience; an efficient workflow can still offer nothing
worth returning for. For a new experience, shape that relationship around a
real occasion, the person's current alternative and the result worth keeping,
and take its character from the practice it serves. For an existing
experience, find the relationship that limits this particular journey. Neither
starts from a generic ideal of fewer steps or more engagement.

A single defect with an evident cause, such as a mislabeled control, a missing
empty state or a lost draft, is fixed where it is, with its reason in a
sentence. Work inside an established product starts from the words, patterns
and expectations its people already hold. Following the whole journey is for
designing how people reach, trust and keep a result, or understanding why they
do not; choosing a direction is for a new product, or for a journey whose form
no longer fits its occasion.

## Follow the payoff

Take a real occasion and follow the person from the need to the result they
keep. Include what happens outside the interface when it determines success:
an exported document must be usable by its receiver; booking is not the same
as completing a visit. The current alternative reveals what the product must
improve and what it must preserve.

Different failures can produce the same apparent abandonment. People may not
value the result, understand the offer, know what to do, trust the consequence,
or be able to finish. Their remedies differ. A clearer label helps a mistaken
prediction; it cannot supply a missing capability. Removing setup helps when
effort precedes value; it backfires when setup establishes a constraint the
person needs before investing further.

Look at the transition where expectation and reality diverge. Marketing,
onboarding, controls, feedback and the retained result jointly create that
expectation. Improving one while leaving another contradictory often shifts
the confusion rather than resolving it.

## Take the character from the practice

A product has character when its decisions come from the work it serves and
would have come out differently for other work. Their sources are in the
person's world rather than in the product's category:

- **The occasion.** What the person is doing when they reach for the product,
  who else is present and what is at stake: a cook at the pass in the middle of
  service, friends setting their fantasy-football lineups before the round
  kicks off, someone closing the accounts of a parent who has died.
- **The objects they already handle.** Tickets on a rail, a crate of records, a
  binder of trading cards, last year's form with its figures. Their shape and
  handling suggest the product's: a rail is read at a glance and cleared by
  touch; a crate is browsed by ear and a few records pulled out to compare.
- **The words they already use**, taken up under "Words are the map" below.
- **The rhythm of the work.** Open all day, used in thirty-second bursts,
  driven by a weekly deadline, resumed once a year, or running in the
  background while the person does something else. What to restore, what to
  show together and when to interrupt follow from that rhythm, together with
  the device's input and the attention the person has to give.

Let those particulars change what the product does, not only what it is
called. Renaming a generic "project" to "set" leaves the template intact; a DJ
preparing a set judges by ear, so hearing two of their own tracks blended
comes before anything is organised. Where the current alternative already has
a form people know how to use, keep that form and remove its pain.

The value people keep can be reliability, relief, confidence, expression or
pleasure, and the practice decides which. Spend the product's emphasis where
that promise becomes visible, and keep the path around that moment quiet,
exact and conventional, because familiar controls carry meaning that novelty
would cost. The kitchen screen spends it on a ticket legible at arm's length
that shows how long it has waited; a service used in grief spends it on calm
and on one plain view of what is done and what remains; a long journey may
need several related moments. Delight scattered over toasts, empty states and
completions reads as decoration, and delight that interrupts the job or makes
effects unpredictable spends the trust it was meant to create. Continuity is
also made of many small, unremarkable choices across the journey; a signature
moment does not replace them.

## Defaults that arrive whatever the product

A request for onboarding, engagement, retention or an assistant can be
answered with the same kits whatever the product. The list below is an
inference about those kits, not a measured ranking, and it shifts with the
model and the year. Each fits some occasion; it is a default rather than a
choice when it would come out unchanged for a different practice:

- **A tour and checklist first run**: welcome modal, tooltip tour, progress
  bar, confetti. Each part has its own case: teaching in use fits a genuinely
  unfamiliar interaction, a checklist fits setup whose parts are all required,
  a progress bar fits progress that means something, and celebration fits an
  achievement the person values. As a kit it explains the interface instead of
  reaching a result, and lists optional work as unfinished.
- **A mascot empty state** with "Get started". Illustration fits a brand people
  already enjoy, or an emptiness that is an achievement, such as a rail cleared
  at the end of service. On first launch the space still has to show what will
  fill it and the action that fills it.
- **Streaks, badges and notification nudges.** A streak fits consistency the
  person chose; a badge fits an achievement the practice already values, such
  as a climber's first send of a grade; a reminder fits a deadline, result or
  next step the person wants to hear about. None follows from a retention
  goal, which adds an obligation the occasion does not have.
- **A chat box with suggestion chips** as the answer to every assistant. It
  fits when the conversation is the job, as in open inquiry or spoken practice;
  when the result is an artifact or a queue of decisions, that belongs at the
  center.
- **A setting offered in place of a decision.** It fits a stable preference
  that genuinely differs among people. A toggle between two conflicting needs
  usually postpones a decision that a better default, a different place for the
  control or a design serving both would settle.
- **A dashboard home** of stat cards and recent activity. It fits when changes
  across several things decide what the person does next; elsewhere the
  opening restores the object, the case or the next action.
- **A persona, a five-stage journey map and findings sorted by heuristic.**
  They fit a team aligning many touchpoints, and a heuristic report is the
  answer when a diagnosis is what was asked for; as the answer to a design
  decision they replace the decision with a catalogue.

When the person or an established product asks for one of these, it is the
answer. Where the brief leaves the choice open, spend it on what the practice
calls for. [Default kits](references/default-kits.md) says where each kit
fits and where it stands in for design, with the evidence there is and
contrasts written with real content; open it when asked for onboarding,
engagement, retention or an assistant.

## Commit to a direction, then test it

When the direction is open, state it as decisions concrete enough to be
wrong, for the part of the product the request leaves open: the occasion and
the alternative it replaces, the first result worth keeping described with
real content, what the product decides so the person need not, the words for
its main objects and actions, where it spends its emphasis, how first use,
return and ending go, and what stays conventional. "Simple, friendly and
powerful" is not yet a direction; "during service the screen is the ticket
rail and nothing else competes with it" is.

Then ask whether the same plan would have come out for a sibling brief. A
booking flow for a tattoo studio that would serve a dentist unchanged has
probably missed something: a studio that quotes custom work from reference
images, takes a deposit and approves a stencil on the day needs those
commitments to appear in the journey. Where the plan matches its sibling,
decide whether that part is shared because it serves both, like a date picker
or a payment form, which should stay familiar, or because the product has no
opinion there yet. Ask again of the first rendered or prototyped result.
[Character sources](references/character-sources.md) gives worked translations
from a practice to product decisions for several dissimilar products, each set
beside a sibling; open it when a new product's first plan reads like its
category.

Show a proposed experience at the moments needed to judge it, with real
content and the exact words. For a new product those are the first screen
holding this person's material, the action and its confirmation, the
hand-off, the result as its receiver sees it and the return after a gap; for a
narrower question, the action and its recovery or one hand-off may be all.
"Project 1", "[CTA]" and placeholder text let a generic plan pass because they
hide whether the hierarchy and the words work for this material.

## Words are the map

The nouns and verbs an interface uses are the model people learn of it. Take
them from the practice when its practitioners are the users: a kitchen screen
says "86 the halibut", "fire table 12" and "four risotto all day", not "mark
unavailable", "start order" and "item count". An Italian fantasy-football
league holds an *asta* and sets each *formazione*; "draft" and "lineup" would
translate away words its players already shout across the room. When the
product serves newcomers to a practice, introduce its words where they are
first needed, beside what they mean. Use the person's word where it names a
concept the product already has. When the work depends on a distinction the
product cannot represent, that is a missing concept for
`charter-functional-design`, not a label to change.

Keep the set of nouns small, and let an action keep its name through its
button, its progress, its confirmation and its history: "Fire table 12" leads
to "Table 12 fired", not to "Order updated". An error says what happened to
the person's work and what they can do now. Precise names beat clever ones.
Take the voice from the occasion and the people using it. State money, health
consequences and commitments precisely, even in a playful product; warmth and
play elsewhere need not make consequential wording ambiguous. Sentence-level
writing belongs to `charter-adaptive-communication`; choosing the vocabulary
and where it appears belongs to the experience.

## First use, return and ending

These three moments teach people what to expect from a product, and each is
designed for its occasion rather than taken from a template.

First use reaches the first result worth keeping, with the person's own
material where the job allows: the DJ hears two tracks from their own library
blended; a party game on the living-room television has every phone in the
room joined by a short code before anyone creates an account. A constraint
that decides whether the result will be usable comes before investment, as
when a clinic that needs a referral says so before the booking form; optional
setup comes after the first result.

Return follows the rhythm. A tool used all day resumes where the hands left it,
without a greeting; a weekly practice opens on what changed since last week; a
yearly task reconstructs what the person has forgotten, such as last year's
answers, what changed in the rules and what remains, with old answers kept
apart from current requirements. Returning becomes more useful through
retained work, reusable context, learned preferences or collaboration where
these belong to the occasion. History that cannot be searched and
notifications that add obligation do not necessarily compound value. Preserve
correction and export; difficulty leaving is captivity, not desire. Low
frequency is not always failure: a yearly administrative tool can succeed by
being easy to resume after a long absence.

The ending is what the person carries out of the product or hands to someone
else: the set exported in the format the venue's players read, cue points
intact; aftercare in hand before the person leaves the studio, with the
follow-up they chose; a stable record that the job is done with nothing
further asked. A celebration fits an achievement the person values; it cannot
stand in for the result or suggest that an unresolved commitment is settled.
If the occasion itself has little value, more onboarding polish will not create
a strategy.

## Ease, control and competence

Before designing controls, decide which choices the product can make well and
which need the person's judgment. Technical configurability does not by itself
create a user decision. Useful ease removes work the person does not value
while preserving their decisions about intent, commitments and consent.

A dependable default with reachable adjustment suits an occasional preference;
a decision about this operation's commitment belongs at its action; choices
people frequently vary or compare belong in the working view. Show the current
destination, scope or cost when it changes what the action commits to. That
visible value does not require a permanently open editor. If input or setup is
required to proceed, make the next step discoverable with the action rather
than hiding a prerequisite among optional settings.

Control is practical: people can change the right input, understand scope,
interrupt the right operation and recover their work. Undo is powerful because
it makes exploration less costly, but only when it actually restores the
relevant state. Regenerating an answer does not undo an external action.

Design for increasing competence. A novice may need examples and a clear first
path; an expert may need dense comparison, shortcuts and batch work. One does
not require degrading the other. Clear hierarchy can reveal an entry route
within a rich workspace. A sparse sequence can be harder than a dense table
when the job requires comparison.

After an action, keep the object available when inspection continues; advance
when processing the next item is the work. Preserve the selection and context
needed for either.

## Conversational and agentic products

When a product converses or acts for someone, the journey is also an exchange
of control, and each hand-off is a transition that can fail.

Choose the center of gravity from the promised job. A conversation-first
product needs a prominent composer and a readable response stream; an
artifact-first product puts the document, canvas or code in the middle with
conversation beside it; a supervision product may rightly center a queue of
work awaiting decisions. Tables, timelines, editable artifacts and selection
controls often beat repeated prose for comparison and precise change, and
context should follow the person across them rather than be explained again.
Turns follow the work, not the chat: people can add or redirect while a run
continues, and a clarifying question leaves independent work usable instead of
holding everything behind one answer.

Set expectations where the person decides what to ask. Say what the system can
do, what it cannot reach and how dependable it is for this kind of request, in
the task's terms: "drafts replies from this inbox's last month" teaches a scope
that "your AI assistant" hides. Example requests built from the person's own
material teach the boundary faster than a capability list, and an overstated
promise turns the first miss into doubt about everything else the system does.
Keep the current destination and scope, such as which project or account a
request will act on, visible where the request is made.

Show the state of delegated work where it changes what the person can do.
Natural language easily blurs a proposal into an effect, so proposed, accepted,
running, awaiting permission, complete and failed need to be distinguishable.
Progress belongs near the work it describes and is never the only record of a
durable result. An interrupted answer should not look settled, a changed
artifact may need a diff or version, and a long operation needs an identity and
a route to its result after the window closes. Narrated internal reasoning is
not progress. When the display itself misleads, the state model belongs to
`charter-status-interface-design`.

Correction reaches the actual work: editing an output, narrowing scope,
stopping further actions, recovering an artifact or changing a preference. A
stop control has a scope (stop speaking, generating or waiting, request remote
cancellation, or prevent further steps) and the product says which one it
performed; "stopped" while an external effect continues is worse than no
control. A rating can feed model improvement, but it is not a repair path and
not every AI interface needs one.

Hand control back where the person's judgment is needed, with what that
judgment needs. A permission request names the object and the consequence, such
as "send these twelve invoices to the accountant," not a generic approval badge.
After a correction, permission or setup step, the interrupted work resumes
without the request being typed again. When a run ends or stalls, the person
finds what was done, what is pending and what they must decide, with the result
still editable.

Aim for reliance that matches reliability, not maximum trust: over-trust lets
errors through unchecked, and under-trust makes people redo work the system did
well. Keep generated, retrieved, inferred and user-authored material
distinguishable where reliance depends on it; show sources where a claim will
be checked or reused, and signal uncertainty where it changes what the person
should verify rather than hedging everything. Memory and standing automations
need a place where people can see and change them. When a model or its behavior
changes, learned calibration may no longer hold. Explain material changes in
terms of what people rely on, rather than treating every model update as proof
that every familiar behavior changed.

## Read the feedback at the right scale

A walkthrough can expose a dead end, ambiguous state or lost draft. To
understand what people value or misunderstand, observe their own attempts and
language without teaching the intended path. Hesitation, workarounds and
recovery are often more informative than a completion checkbox.

Completion, satisfaction, voluntary return, payment and business retention
answer different questions. Faster completion may reflect improved flow or
early abandonment; a low click rate may mean a needless action disappeared.
Use the denominator and the user occasion to interpret the measure.

Choose the change that addresses the limiting mechanism and watch its plausible
cost: a helpful default may conceal scope; assistance may interrupt focus;
simplification may impair expert work. Let what happens at that seam change
the design. Return the improved experience or the requested actionable design,
shown at its decisive moments, not a tour of every heuristic.
