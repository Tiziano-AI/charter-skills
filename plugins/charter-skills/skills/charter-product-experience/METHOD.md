# Charter · Product Experience

An experience is the relationship between the result people seek, the work a
product asks of them, and what they learn to expect from it. A beautiful screen
can sit inside a bad experience; an efficient workflow can still offer nothing
worth returning for. For a new experience, shape that relationship around a
real occasion, the person's current alternative and the result worth keeping.
For an existing experience, find the relationship that limits this particular
journey. Neither starts from a generic ideal of fewer steps or more engagement.

A single defect with an evident cause, such as a mislabeled control, a missing
empty state or a lost draft, is fixed where it is, with its reason in a
sentence. Following the whole journey is for designing how people reach,
trust and keep a result, or understanding why they do not.

## Follow the payoff

Take a real occasion and follow the person from the need to the result they
keep. Include what happens outside the interface when it determines success:
an exported document must be usable by its receiver; booking is not the same
as completing a visit. The current alternative reveals what the product must
improve and what it must preserve.

Different failures can produce the same apparent abandonment. People may not
value the result, understand the offer, know what to do, trust the consequence,
or be able to finish. Their remedies differ. A clearer label helps a mistaken
prediction; it cannot supply a missing capability. Removing setup helps when
effort precedes value; it backfires when setup establishes a constraint the
person needs before investing further.

Look at the transition where expectation and reality diverge. Marketing,
onboarding, controls, feedback and the retained result jointly create that
expectation. Improving one while leaving another contradictory often shifts
the confusion rather than resolving it.

## Ease, control and competence

Before designing controls, decide which choices the product can make well and
which need the person's judgment. Technical configurability does not by itself
create a user decision. Useful ease removes work the person does not value
while preserving their decisions about intent, commitments and consent.

A dependable default with reachable adjustment suits an occasional preference;
a decision about this operation's commitment belongs at its action; choices
people frequently vary or compare belong in the working view. Show the current
destination, scope or cost when it changes what the action commits to. That
visible value does not require a permanently open editor. If input or setup is
required to proceed, make the next step discoverable with the action rather
than hiding a prerequisite among optional settings.

Control is practical: people can change the right input, understand scope,
interrupt the right operation and recover their work. Undo is powerful because
it makes exploration less costly, but only when it actually restores the
relevant state. Regenerating an answer does not undo an external action.

Design for increasing competence. A novice may need examples and a clear first
path; an expert may need dense comparison, shortcuts and batch work. One does
not require degrading the other. Clear hierarchy can reveal an entry route
within a rich workspace. A sparse sequence can be harder than a dense table
when the job requires comparison.

## Conversational and agentic products

When a product converses or acts for someone, the journey is also an exchange
of control, and each hand-off is a transition that can fail.

Choose the center of gravity from the promised job. A conversation-first
product needs a prominent composer and a readable response stream; an
artifact-first product puts the document, canvas or code in the middle with
conversation beside it; a supervision product may rightly center a queue of
work awaiting decisions. Tables, timelines, editable artifacts and selection
controls often beat repeated prose for comparison and precise change, and
context should follow the person across them rather than be explained again.
Turns follow the work, not the chat: people can add or redirect while a run
continues, and a clarifying question leaves independent work usable instead of
holding everything behind one answer.

Set expectations where the person decides what to ask. Say what the system can
do, what it cannot reach and how dependable it is for this kind of request, in
the task's terms: "drafts replies from this inbox's last month" teaches a scope
that "your AI assistant" hides. Example requests teach the boundary faster than
a capability list, and an overstated promise turns the first miss into doubt
about everything else the system does. Keep the current destination and scope,
such as which project or account a request will act on, visible where the
request is made.

Show the state of delegated work where it changes what the person can do.
Natural language easily blurs a proposal into an effect, so proposed, accepted,
running, awaiting permission, complete and failed need to be distinguishable.
Progress belongs near the work it describes and is never the only record of a
durable result. An interrupted answer should not look settled, a changed
artifact may need a diff or version, and a long operation needs an identity and
a route to its result after the window closes. Narrated internal reasoning is
not progress. When the display itself misleads, the state model belongs to
`charter-status-interface-design`.

Correction reaches the actual work: editing an output, narrowing scope,
stopping further actions, recovering an artifact or changing a preference. A
stop control has a scope (stop speaking, generating or waiting, request remote
cancellation, or prevent further steps) and the product says which one it
performed; "stopped" while an external effect continues is worse than no
control. A rating can feed model improvement, but it is not a repair path and
not every AI interface needs one.

Hand control back where the person's judgment is needed, with what that
judgment needs. A permission request names the object and the consequence, such
as "send these twelve invoices to the accountant," not a generic approval badge.
After a correction, permission or setup step, the interrupted work resumes
without the request being typed again. When a run ends or stalls, the person
finds what was done, what is pending and what they must decide, with the result
still editable.

Aim for reliance that matches reliability, not maximum trust: over-trust lets
errors through unchecked, and under-trust makes people redo work the system did
well. Keep generated, retrieved, inferred and user-authored material
distinguishable where reliance depends on it; show sources where a claim will
be checked or reused, and signal uncertainty where it changes what the person
should verify rather than hedging everything. Memory and standing automations
need a place where people can see and change them. When a model or its behavior
changes, learned calibration may no longer hold. Explain material changes in
terms of what people rely on, rather than treating every model update as proof
that every familiar behavior changed.

## Give the product a reason to be kept

Reliability, relief, expression, confidence and satisfaction can each be part
of value. Craft gives those qualities perceptible form: a deliberate voice,
a readable rhythm, responsive manipulation, well-judged motion, and a result
that feels cared for. Delight that interrupts the job or makes effects
unpredictable spends the trust it was meant to create.

Returning should become more useful through retained work, reusable context,
learned preferences or collaboration where these belong to the occasion.
History that cannot be searched and notifications that add obligation do not
necessarily compound value. Preserve correction and export; difficulty leaving
is captivity, not desire.

Low frequency is not always failure. A yearly administrative tool can succeed
by being easy to resume after a long absence. If the occasion itself has little
value, more onboarding polish will not create a strategy.

## Read the feedback at the right scale

A walkthrough can expose a dead end, ambiguous state or lost draft. To
understand what people value or misunderstand, observe their own attempts and
language without teaching the intended path. Hesitation, workarounds and
recovery are often more informative than a completion checkbox.

Completion, satisfaction, voluntary return, payment and business retention
answer different questions. Faster completion may reflect improved flow or
early abandonment; a low click rate may mean a needless action disappeared.
Use the denominator and the user occasion to interpret the measure.

Choose the change that addresses the limiting mechanism and watch its plausible
cost: a helpful default may conceal scope; assistance may interrupt focus;
simplification may impair expert work. Let what happens at that seam change
the design. Return the improved experience or the requested actionable design,
not a tour of every heuristic.
