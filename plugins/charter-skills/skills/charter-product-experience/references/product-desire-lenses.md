# When a useful product still lacks pull

Usability explains whether people can use a product. It does not by itself
explain why they choose it, return to it or feel it is worth keeping.

## Occasion and payoff

Look for a concrete recurring occasion and the value the person wants then.
Weak repeat use can be correct for an infrequent job. Do not manufacture daily
engagement as the success criterion for a product needed once a year.

Compare the experienced payoff with the existing alternative, including doing
nothing. Faster setup may help trial without changing the reason to switch.
A more polished result may matter greatly when it represents the user to
someone else. Follow value into that real context.

## Confidence, competence and expression

A product can create desire by letting people do something previously hard,
feel capable, express taste or identity, or trust an important result.
Investigate which of these belongs to the actual user rather than adding
generic delight.

Craft can give a mundane activity a satisfying rhythm: clear state changes,
precise direct manipulation, appropriate motion, consistent sound or tactile
feedback, and an ending that leaves useful work in hand. Surprise should not
make important behavior unpredictable.

Autonomy matters alongside ease. Assistance that saves steps but removes
understanding or editability may feel less valuable than a slightly slower
tool the person controls.

## Accumulated value without captivity

Saved context, organized history, reusable work, learned preferences and
collaboration can make the next use better. Identify what actually compounds.
A growing list of notifications or an unsearchable conversation history may
increase burden rather than value.

Preserve export, correction and understandable ownership. Difficulty leaving
is not evidence of desire; coercive defaults or anxiety about losing progress
can produce activity while reducing trust.

## Observe rather than ascribe

Ask about the last real occasion and observe the next one when possible.
Separate stated enthusiasm, willingness to invest effort, voluntary return,
payment and recommendation; each has its own alternative explanations.

“Relief,” “confidence,” “fatigue” and “delight” are hypotheses unless supported
by the person's report or behavior. In studied bounded experiences, peaks and
endings can weigh heavily in later evaluations, but their importance varies
with the kind of experience and the way it is judged. That finding does not
establish what determines this product's retention or recommendation. Inspect
whether a painful moment or failed ending actually shapes what people remember
and do. A good ending still does not repair a journey that failed the result:
the person did the work and lacks what they came for. Improve the result people
keep, not just the story the interface tells at completion.

The resulting choice may be a stronger payoff, better repeat continuity,
distinctive craft, reduced interruption, or a strategy change because the
occasion is not valuable enough. Do not polish your way around the last one.
