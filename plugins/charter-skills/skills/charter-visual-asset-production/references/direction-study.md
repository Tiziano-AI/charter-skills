# Direction study: three pictures, and one picture in three grades

A long magazine feature on a working bell foundry needs a 3:2 landscape
cover, and its look is open. The foundry is fictional and unnamed; the
practice is real. Every image is AI-generated concept work, not a record of
a foundry. The [comparison plate](../assets/direction-study/plate.jpg) shows
three pictures above and one picture in three grades below.

## The metal is the light

[The pour](../assets/direction-study/pour.jpg) makes weight and heat the
subject: a massive black ladle releases a thin bright thread. The photographic
manner holds ordinary refractory grit and soot against liquid bronze, without
a spark shower. The viewer stands laterally at chest height, about 2.5 metres
from the pour. At night, the metal in the ladle and its falling stream cast
low orange light across the pouring cup and sand; the room falls into black.
The palette comes from molten bronze, black iron, grey workwear and soot.

The direction takes its idea from casting through an inlet into the closed
core-and-cope assembly. The bell is hidden inside the mould. Heat is the
source of the light, not a warm grade applied to an unrelated workshop view.

## The curve makes the bell

[The mould](../assets/direction-study/form.jpg) makes measured form the
subject. A two-ink relief-print manner uses flat silhouettes, carved gaps and
sweeping marks rather than photographic shading with paper texture. The
viewer meets the mouth-down loam core nearly square-on at the moulder's eye
height, about two metres away. A mid-morning doorway at left supplies a hard
shadow to the right, translated into printed planes. Graphite black belongs
to iron and shadow; oxide red belongs to the earth; cool grey is the stock.

The central spindle and profiled strickle turn the relationship between tool
and contour into the picture. Loam moulding supplies this direction, not a
claim that every present-day foundry uses the same mould materials.

## A note is cut away

[The tuning view](../assets/direction-study/sound.jpg) makes subtraction and
listening the subject. The photographic manner is quiet and close: a huge
cropped cavity, concentric cutting marks, a small chip tray and a mallet at
the far rim. The viewer looks steeply down from a raised position about two
metres away. Broad cool noon skylight from upper left describes the bronze
interior. Dull cut bronze, worn black-green machine paint, a grey wall and an
indigo sleeve supply the palette; there is no furnace glow.

The bell sits mouth-up on a stopped vertical tuning lathe. Selective removal
from its inside changes its partials; the hum, prime, tierce, quint and nominal
are five prominent ones. The listening pause follows the cut. Fettling and
facing belong between casting and tuning, outside these three chosen moments.

## One picture, three treatments

The [untinted picture](../assets/direction-study/expected-source.jpg) is one
response to the bare request for a bell-foundry cover. Its proposition is
“Foundry as spectacle”: a pour, workers and finished bells in a lit workshop.
One sample suggests an undirected answer, not a measured generator default.
The [blue](../assets/direction-study/expected-blue.jpg),
[gold](../assets/direction-study/expected-gold.jpg) and
[teal](../assets/direction-study/expected-teal.jpg) versions use the same
luminance plane with three deterministic colour mappings. Their moment,
viewpoint, people and machinery stay in exactly the same positions.

These are one direction, not three answers to an open picture idea. They are
the right comparison when that picture is settled and only its colour
treatment is open. The [study notes](../assets/direction-study/README.md)
contain the prompts, colour operation, practice sources and provenance limits.

## Look is not construction truth

The takes settle proposition, composition, manner, light and palette. The
pour's suspension and mould assembly remain partly hidden; the print
simplifies the strickle linkage and profile; the tuning crop hides clamping
and tool support. Its turning bands prescribe no actual tuning cuts. These
invented forms are not engineering drawings or evidence of safe practice.

A cover for a feature on a real foundry uses photographs made there, with
the chosen moment checked against its actual work. A generated photograph
published as that foundry claims a fidelity it cannot have.
