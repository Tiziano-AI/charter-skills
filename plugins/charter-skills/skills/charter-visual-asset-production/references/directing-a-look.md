# Directing a look

Open this when you write the direction for new imagery or motion: a concept
take, a single illustration, a campaign family or a shot. It works briefs from
very different worlds into decisions that a generator, a photographer or an
illustrator can act on; it lists answers worth checking for when those
decisions are left open; and it gives craft particulars with the conditions
that change them. The selected capability's own guide still says how to phrase
a request to it. This says what to ask for.

## From a brief to visible decisions

Each case starts from the words a brief usually arrives in, finds where the
look comes from, and ends with the nearby case that changes the answer.

**A lucha libre night in Veracruz.** "Something loud for Saturday's fights."
A source for the look is the letterpress posters documented at Imprenta Enlace
in Xalapa: several wood and metal typefaces on one sheet, their variety
marking the hierarchy of the information, colours in gradients, images cut in
high contrast, absorbent paper. Look at the actual posters and take the
relationships that serve this event. The medium is type-led print with one
figure. The wrestlers' names, the date and the venue change every week and
must be exact, so they are set as text in an editable source, in the
organiser's own Spanish wording with its accents, and checked for line breaks
at print size; a drawn or generated figure becomes one component of it. Loud
here means many typefaces and hard contrast. The nearby case is a televised
league with sponsors and its own identity system: the system governs, and
decides how much of the street poster it can borrow.

**Core photographs for an exploration report.** "Photos of this quarter's
drill core." The look is the field's own protocol: each tray shot straight
down from a fixed distance with a lens that does not distort, under even light
with high colour rendering, a scale bar, a colour chart and the hole and tray
label in frame, so that readers can compare grain and colour between trays and
holes. The medium is capture under repeatable exposure, light and white
balance; the colour chart in frame is what lets a documented correction bring
trays shot on different days to the same colour, with the captured original
kept. A generated image, or a grade applied for looks, would destroy the
evidence, and distinctiveness is not a criterion. The nearby case is the
report's cover, where atmosphere is the role: the rig at first light or the
racks of the core shed receding into the dark open the direction again.

**Fire equipment in an engine room.** "A poster showing the crew where the fire
gear is." The look comes from the vessel's own approved fire-control plan,
which already fixes where each item is and how it is drawn. The medium is a
diagram, and the poster carries over that plan's symbols, labels, orientation
and colour meanings: shipboard plans have their own symbol and layout system
(ISO 17631, with the IMO's fire-control-plan symbols), which is not the system
of the signs mounted on the bulkheads (ISO 7010). The design work is
legibility at the distance the crew reads it from: the arrangement in thin
grey line, the symbols large enough to read, colour kept for the meanings the
plan gives it. Where the plan or a location is unconfirmed, the poster leaves
it out rather than inventing equipment or a code. The nearby case is a
recruitment film for the same shipping line, which wants the engine room's
heat, noise and scale: a photographed person among the machinery, lit by the
room's own fittings, carries that and a diagram cannot.

**An aso-ebi invitation.** "An invitation for my parents' fortieth anniversary
in Lagos." At many Yoruba celebrations the hosts choose a cloth, aso-ebi,
that family and friends wear to show they belong to the occasion. Where the
hosts have chosen one, the invitation can take that cloth as its ground,
photographed flat at true colour, and its two or three colours as the palette,
with the couple's names large and a photograph of the couple themselves. It is
dense and celebratory by right. The nearby case is a cloth not yet chosen: its
look is not available to borrow, and the invitation either waits for it or
leads with the couple's photograph.

**A cable-car pictogram in an existing transit set.** "Add the new cable-car
line to the station signs." The set owns the style: its grid, stroke weight,
corner radius, viewing angle and colour. The subject's world supplies only what
makes the sign recognisable at a glance, the cabin hanging from a single arm
under its cable. The conventional silhouette is the right answer here, and
personality beyond it is noise. The nearby case is the poster announcing the
line, where the city seen from the cabin can carry an image of its own.

### Three directions, and one direction tinted three ways

A long feature on a dawn tuna auction in Tokyo needs a cover image, and its
look is open. Three directions that differ in what the picture is:

- **The room.** From the buyers' line at their eye level, with a long lens that
  stacks the rows of frozen fish under the hall's flat overhead light; one
  buyer's torch on a cut tail, the auctioneer mid-call at the far end. The
  palette is frost, red flesh and rubber boots.
- **The cut.** One cross-section of a tail, photographed straight down on
  steel under low raking light so that the grain and the fat lines show. The
  red of the flesh and the grey of the steel are the whole palette.
- **The marks.** Close views of the red characters and numbers painted on the
  frozen flanks, used as the feature's lettering, with the fish as their
  ground.

One direction tinted three ways is the same centred fish on crushed ice in a
blue grade, a gold grade and a teal grade. That offers a treatment decision,
useful once the picture is chosen. Here the picture is what is open, so the
person can choose only a colour, and the room, the cut and the marks go
unexplored.

Because the feature reports a real place, the three are directions for a
photographer, and proposals to check against what is really there: confirm how
buyers inspect the tails and what is painted on the fish before relying on
either. Generated takes can settle which one to shoot, but a generated auction
published as the auction claims a fidelity it cannot have.

The [direction study](direction-study.md) shows the same distinction in
pictures for another commission, a cover for a feature on a bell foundry.

## Defaults and when they are right

These are answers worth checking for at present when a generator is given an
open choice: examples from published studies of default images and from
inference, not a ranking of how often each occurs. They shift with each model
and year, differ between generators and samples, and come back more often for
short or vague prompts and unfamiliar words. Each is right for some brief, so
the question is whether this brief chose it.

| What can come back when the choice is open | What it displaces | Right when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A centred subject at eye level in a medium shot | A viewpoint chosen for what the picture says: low to make it loom, high to show its place, close to put the viewer among things | An identity photo, or a product grid or comparison set whose members must align |
| Shallow focus against a soft blur or gradient | The subject's place, which says where and when | Isolation serves the expression or object and the place is not needed, the place would mislead or distract, or the layout supplies its own ground |
| Warm golden or teal-and-orange light with a rim and haze | Light with a source, an hour and a quality from the subject's place | The subject really happens at dusk, or the grade is the film's or campaign's chosen intent |
| Glossy, evenly sharp detail and poreless skin | A hierarchy of detail, and surfaces with their real wear, grain and response | A new object's hero image where finish is what is sold; for skin, a beauty manner the brief has chosen |
| A product floating above a reflective plinth | The product in use, at its scale, in its place | A launch image where the form is the news |
| Friendly illustration as pastel clay-like 3D or flat figures with oversized limbs | A manner drawn from the subject's world or its brand | The brand or commission has chosen that manner |
| The category sign: a padlock, a brain, a lightbulb, a leaf, a handshake | A particular relationship in the subject | Icons and navigation, where convention reads fastest |
| Unfamiliar words turned into familiar fantasy motifs such as floating islands, birds or butterflies | The unfamiliar thing described by its material, size and use | Those motifs belong to the fantasy the brief intends; a real unfamiliar subject needs a reference or a description instead |
| A polished, idealised finish whatever was asked, so that requested roughness, ugliness or discomfort comes back smoothed | The roughness the brief asked for | The brief asked for polish; when it asked for roughness, the smoothing is a miss to correct |
| A style word added to an otherwise default scene | A manner chosen for its behaviour, with the composition and moment changed to suit it | The scene is right and only its manner is open |
| In video, a slow push-in or orbit, everything drifting, particles, uniform slow motion | One motivated move, or a still camera with an action at its own pace | An ambient loop or background plate whose purpose is atmosphere and whose movement does not compete with text or controls in front of it |

Describe the scene that should be there before what to leave out: "an empty,
deserted street" says what the frame holds, where "no cars" leaves the
generator to fill the street with something else. Unwanted lettering, logos or
extra subjects are worth naming when they would be plausible additions,
together with what the space holds instead; where the selected capability has
its own exclusion or negative controls, use them for that.

## Craft particulars

### Light

Name light by its source, direction, hour and quality. Hardness follows the
source's apparent size as seen from the subject: the noon sun is small and
casts hard shadows, while an overcast sky or a large window close by is broad
and soft.

Low light from the side, raking across a surface, reveals relief: carving,
bread crust, brushwork, weave, a worn step. Even frontal light suppresses
relief, which is right when colour must be compared or texture would distract.
Flat, matt artwork is commonly reproduced with two matched lights placed
symmetrically at about 45 degrees to the surface, so that reflections bounce
away from the lens; adjust the angle and diffusion for even coverage.
Cross-polarising the lights and the lens suppresses the glare that remains,
and can also change how sheen and colour appear, so compare the result with
the original and a colour target. Glossy, metallic or heavily textured work
may need another arrangement to keep the surface information that polarising
removes.

A polished surface shows its surroundings more than its own colour, so its
form is drawn largely by the shape of the reflections you give it. Glass is
outlined either dark against a bright field or bright against a dark one; both
draw its contour, so choose by which shows the edges, colour and thickness the
picture needs.

Low-pressure sodium street lamps give almost a single wavelength of
yellow-orange light, near 589 nm, under which colours collapse into one hue.
Check which lighting the place had in the year depicted before using it as a
period or place signal; where white LEDs have replaced sodium, the street no
longer has that colour.

### Viewpoint and distance

Perspective comes from where the camera stands, and the lens frames it.
Standing far back with a long lens stacks a crowd into a wall and makes a
distant skyline loom behind a figure; standing close with a wide lens swells
whatever is nearest and puts the viewer among things.

Eye level tends to read as meeting an equal. A low, close camera can make the
subject loom; a high one shows layout and makes figures small in their place.
A face that fills the frame is intimate or confrontational; a figure small in
a wide field makes the picture about the place. These are conventions, so read
the effect in the actual frame.

### Palette and value

Take a few colours from the subject's matter and let one carry the accent.
Check the value structure before adding colour: shrink the image to thumbnail
size or view it in greyscale, and where the subject is meant to read by light
and dark and does not, more colour will not fix it. Where hue carries the
distinction on purpose, as in a colour-coded map, judge the colour image at
display size and under the colour-vision conditions its audience requires. A
quiet region can hold rich detail at low contrast without competing.

A festival market or a toy catalogue can carry many saturated hues when a few
large masses of light and dark hold them together. Colour that already codes a
meaning in the subject's world, such as safety colours, transit lines or a
team's kit, stays reserved for that meaning.

### Surface and wear

Wear says that a thing has been used, so put it where use would put it: scuffs
where hands grip, dust in corners, polish where sleeves rub, a path worn into a
floor. A new product's launch image shows it pristine when newness is the
promise; evidence shows the surface as found; wear added on purpose belongs to
a brief that makes use part of the character.

### Type where copy meets an image

When copy joins an image, its type takes its character from the image's world
or from the role of the publication that receives it: the wood type of a local
poster, a transit system's sign face, a comic's own lettering, a literary face
for an invitation to read. A cream field with a serif headline that ends in
italic under a spaced capital label is one such choice, right for a
reading-led piece; it can also arrive when nothing was decided, so check that
it was chosen for this image. The layout around the image belongs to the
receiving product; [the composition study](composition-and-derivation.md)
shows how panels and copy share a frame.

## Directing motion

For each shot, decide:

- what the viewer understands by its end;
- the principal action, or the coordinated relationship, that carries it, and
  how it moves: its path (a limb swings in an arc, a piston travels straight,
  a wheel turns), its weight, shown through timing and spacing (how long it
  takes to start and stop, how far it travels between frames, what happens on
  impact), and its acceleration (easing in and out, or a hard stop when
  something hits);
- its pace against the length of the shot: an action that fills the shot reads
  as deliberate, one that takes a few frames at its end reads as a surprise;
- what holds still and what moves in support: the camera, the background, the
  other figures. A moving hold, a held pose that keeps breathing or drifting
  slightly, keeps a posed figure alive without competing with the action; a
  fully still hold can suit a graphic or limited manner better.

Anticipation before a move, and follow-through and overlapping action after it,
give the move weight; these come from the principles that Frank Thomas and
Ollie Johnston set out in *The Illusion of Life* (1981), where staging, arcs,
slow in and slow out and secondary action also live. Secondary action supports
the main action or is left out.

Move the camera for a reason: to follow an action, or to reveal a relationship
or scale that the opening frame could not show. Across cuts, keep screen
direction: a subject that leaves frame right enters the next shot from frame
left, and two people facing each other stay on the same sides, unless the
reversal is the point.

A 180-degree shutter exposes each frame for half its interval, 1/48 s at 24
frames per second, and gives the amount of motion blur that cinema convention
has made familiar. A narrower shutter makes crisper, more staccato frames,
which can suit impact or unease, depending on the movement. When the
capability exposes no shutter, judge the blur in the result and describe the
one you want. Hand-drawn animation at 24 frames per second often holds each
drawing for two frames (on twos, twelve drawings a second) and moves fast or
smooth action to every frame (on ones); the mix belongs to the manner, so
decide it for a clip that imitates drawn animation rather than leaving every
frame to move.

In a composited loop, layers that cycle at different lengths delay the moment
the whole frame repeats: 3- and 5-second cycles realign only after 15 seconds.
A short cycle that draws the eye can still read as a repeat, so judge the
whole loop in playback. A loop's seam joins every state that changes, not only
the pose: liquid levels, particles, light and movement. A generated clip loops
as one piece, so its seam is judged on the whole frame.

**A teh tarik clip for a stall's menu screen in Penang.** Six seconds, played
once and cut, because a pour does not loop: the tea has moved from one mug to
the other and the foam has risen, so matching the mugs' positions at both ends
would not match their contents. A short hold shows the two mugs. The tea is
pulled from a raised mug into a low one at arm's length: a slight dip before
the lift, then the stream lengthens as the hand rises and foam builds in the
lower mug. A final hold lets the full mug and its foam register. The
stall, the stacked cups and the camera hold still, and steam drifting slowly
is the one secondary action. The pour fills most of the clip, so it reads as a
skill rather than a blur. For continuous playback the pour repeats behind a
cut, or a separate loop of steam alone runs between pours. The nearby case is
a clip behind the menu's prices, where the pour would pull the eye from the
text; there steam alone is the right movement.

**A market reveal in Kumasi.** The shot holds for a beat on one trader's hands
counting change, then the camera rises at a steady pace until the market's
roofs and lanes fill the frame, and holds long enough for them to read. The
move carries the change from one person to many. The nearby case is a story
about the trader herself: the camera stays with her, and the market arrives as
sound and passers-by.
