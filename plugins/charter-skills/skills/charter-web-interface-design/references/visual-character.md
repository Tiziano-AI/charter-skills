# Visual character in detail

Open this when a new product or page needs a direction of its own, or when an
existing look reads as generic and the complaint is about identity rather
than arrangement. It shows how a subject's particulars become type, colour
and layout, then carries the craft detail for each. The worked subjects are
for open directions; the sections on type, colour, imagery and motion also
serve a local repair that turns on that craft. Work inside an established
system keeps that system's direction.

## From the subject's world to decisions

Start by collecting, not styling. For the subject in front of you, write down
what is actually there:

- **Objects and documents.** What its people handle, fill in, print, pin up or
  point at. Their proportions, their ruling, what is large on them and what is
  small.
- **Lettering and colour already in use.** Signs, labels, uniforms, equipment,
  packaging, the default output of the tools of the trade.
- **Place and light.** Indoors or out, bright or dim, clean or worn, crowded
  or empty.
- **Pace.** A glance while walking, a long evening read, a decision made once
  a year, a check repeated every few minutes.
- **Words.** What the people call things, and what they would never say.

Then choose two or three of those particulars and translate each into a
decision. The subjects below are worked through to show the size of the
step, and how far apart honest answers land. They are illustrations of the
method; none is a look to reuse.

**A municipal swimming pool's timetable and booking page.** Particulars: lane
ropes with their alternating floats, depth figures painted on the pool edge,
the tile grid, the laminated weekly timetable by the entrance, lanes booked
by the session, wet hands on a phone. Decisions: each day is drawn as the
pool itself, one column per lane with time running down its length and
sessions as blocks in their lanes, so a swimmer sees which lane is free at
seven without reading a grid of times, and the week is a row of those pools
in miniature to pick the day from; a white tile ground with a visible square
module that every element snaps to; pool blue as the one large colour field,
with the red-and-white rhythm of a lane rope reserved for the sessions that
are full, which also say so in words; session times in numerals built on the
tile module, the way depth markings are set in tile at the pool edge, large
enough for a glance, and a plain, wide sans for everything else; controls
with generous targets. Displaced: a smiling stock swimmer over a wave
gradient, with three benefit cards.

**A small record label for sound-system music.** Particulars: hand-painted
speaker boxes, seven-inch labels with text set in a ring around the spindle
hole, rubber-stamped catalogue numbers, dance posters in two flat colours of
ink on coloured paper, bass you feel before you hear. Decisions: a saturated
flat ground taken from the sleeve of the record now playing, with each
release's own colour on its label; heavy wood-type capitals for name, artist
and title, set tight, the label's name filling the measure from edge to edge
as a poster printer fits a line; each release drawn as its own label, in the
round, and the catalogue is a wall of those discs;
the catalogue number set like a stamp because it is one; the player fixed to
the bottom edge like a deck, clear of the content and of focused controls at
narrow widths, and the only motion on the page is the disc that is playing.
This lands close to the poster look described below; here the label's own
dance posters asked for it. Displaced: a near-black page with a neon accent
and a grid of square cover images.

**A seismology lab's public page for recent earthquakes.** Particulars: the
drum recorder's parallel traces, station codes, magnitude and depth as the
facts people came for, graph paper, the map with its rings. Decisions: the
live trace from one named station is the identity, drawn across the full
width at the top of the page with its station code and time window, quiet
when the ground is quiet; a calm paper-grey ground so that the one saturated
colour can mean a recent event and nothing else; a plain, legible sans for
everything people read, and a monospace face only for station codes and
times, because they are read character by character; the event list is a
table with aligned figures and their units, newest first, and a selected
event is marked on the map and at its arrival on the trace, which comes later
than the event's origin time and is labelled as an arrival. Displaced: a dark
dashboard of tiles, each with a big number and a sparkline.

**A subtitling tool for film translators.** Particulars: timecodes such as
00:12:41,280 that translators read and type all day, the audio waveform with
each subtitle laid along it as a block, the limit of two short lines per
subtitle, white text with a dark edge over a moving picture, work finished
late at night before a festival screening. Decisions: the opening is one film
frame across the full width with its subtitle being retimed, and the waveform
with its cue blocks beneath it, so the product's own drawing is the image and
no picture of software is needed; the headline is set as a subtitle, two
short lines inside the frame, because that is the form these people spend
their days on; timecodes in a monospace face because they are typed and
compared character by character; elsewhere a plain sans on a neutral grey, as
in an edit suite, so that the picture keeps the colour. Displaced: a white
page with a violet gradient, the app in a tilted laptop frame and three
benefit cards.

**A Persian-language poetry journal.** Particulars: classical verse in lines
of two balanced halves beside free verse with its own lineation, calligraphic
titles, margins that hold commentary, reading from right to left, an audience
that reads slowly and returns. Decisions: the page is built right to left
from the start (`dir="rtl"` and `lang="fa"` on the document, and margins,
alignment and positions written as inline start and end), so navigation and
margins mirror themselves and no left-to-right template is flipped at the
end; each poem keeps its own form, a classical one set as its couplets, the
two halves on one line with a clear gap where the width allows and stacked
where it does not, and free verse in its own lineation; Latin names and
numerals inside the text are marked up to keep their own direction; the
editors' glosses sit in the outer margin beside the couplet they explain, as
commentary sits in a manuscript, not in footnotes; a calligraphic face for
the poem's title only, at a size and line height that give its sloping
strokes room, and a text face designed for continuous Persian reading for
verse and prose, set larger and with more line height than Latin text at the
same nominal size would need, with generous quiet margins. Displaced: a cream
page with a Latin display serif, footnotes, and type sizes carried over from
the English edition that crowd the script.

In each case the test is the same. Describe the page without its name: if the
description would fit a neighbouring subject equally well (any sports
facility, any label, any data service, any software product, any journal),
the particulars have not reached the page yet. Conventions the neighbours
rightly share, a table for events or a form for a booking, are not what the
test is about.

## Type

**Choosing.** Read what a face was made for. Faces drawn for signage, for
small print, for screens, for books and for headlines behave differently at
the same size, and that history is most of their voice. Look at the specific
characters the page will lean on: the figures if the page is numeric, the
capitals if the title is short, the italics if there is quotation, the
diacritics and scripts the content really contains. A family with several
widths or optical sizes can supply display and text roles without a second
family. Where the brand or the platform sets the face, the choices that
remain (weight, size, spacing, measure) still decide whether it looks set or
merely applied.

**Setting text.** A measure of roughly 45 to 75 characters suits continuous
reading in most Latin faces; narrower for captions and side notes, and a
little wider is tolerable with more leading. Line height for body text
usually falls between 1.4 and 1.6 and rises with the measure and with a
face's x-height; long-descender scripts and calligraphic styles need more.
Set the measure in `ch` or by a maximum width, not by the viewport; `ch` is
the width of the zero, which is wider than the average letter in most text
faces, so count a real line and do not trust the number. Use
hyphenation only with the content's language declared, and prefer
`text-wrap: pretty` where it is supported to avoid a short last line.

**Setting display.** Large type needs less of everything: line height near
1.0 to 1.15 (lines in capitals alone, which have no descenders, can go
tighter), tracking slightly negative as size grows, and line breaks chosen by
sense, not by where the box happens to end. `text-wrap: balance` evens a
heading's lines when no break has been chosen by hand; a deliberately uneven
break can be stronger. Capitals and small sizes go the other way and want a
little positive tracking. Scripts whose letters join, such as Arabic, are not
tracked. Optical corrections count at this scale: a round letter or a
quotation mark at the left edge may need to hang slightly outside the margin
to look aligned. Safari supports `hanging-punctuation: first`; elsewhere a
small negative indent on that line does the same.

**Figures and data.** Use tabular, lining figures (`font-variant-numeric:
tabular-nums lining-nums`) wherever numbers are compared or change in place,
and align them on the decimal or the right edge. Tabular figures do not need
a monospace face; reserve one for code, identifiers and measurements, which
are read character by character. A face can offer the feature and still not
deliver equal widths, or deliver them at one weight only: before a column
depends on it, measure two strings of digits of equal length in the page at
each weight in use. In some faces the feature also sets the full stop, comma
and colon to figure width, which opens gaps in running text, so apply it to
the cells and inputs that hold figures, not to the page.

**Loading.** A page designed around a face that fails to load is a different
page. Bundle or host the files where possible, limit the weights to those in
use, set `font-display` deliberately, choose a fallback with similar
proportions (the `size-adjust` and `ascent-override` descriptors on a local
fallback can bring it closer), and confirm in the rendered page which face is
actually drawn, at the narrowest width as well as the widest.
`document.fonts.ready` says a face loaded, not that it drew every glyph: an
arrow or symbol the face lacks comes from a system fallback, and the browser's
developer tools list the fonts actually rendered for each element.

## Colour

Give each colour one job and write the jobs down: ground, text, secondary
text, the colour that carries the identity, action, selection, and the status
colours. A decorative accent and the action colour can be different colours;
when one hue is asked to decorate, to mark selection and to warn, the screen
looks striking and reads ambiguously.

Build tints and shades in a perceptually even space such as OKLCH, so that
equal steps look nearly equal, and let chroma fall toward the lightest and
darkest ends. Derive secondary text and borders from the surface they sit on,
not from a single grey used everywhere: grey text on a coloured ground
usually looks dirty. A dark scheme is composed for darkness (lower chroma on
large areas, elevation shown by lighter surfaces) and is not the light scheme
inverted.

Check contrast where it is decided, against the ground the text actually sits
on. Text needs 4.5:1 or better, and that includes labels, captions,
placeholders and secondary text, which is where pale and tinted palettes
usually fail; text from 24px, or from about 18.7px bold, needs 3:1. The parts
of a control or a chart that someone needs to see to find it and read its
state (a field's edge when nothing else marks the field, a checkbox, a focus
indicator, a chart line) need 3:1 against what surrounds them; a button
identified by its own text needs no outline. Status carried by colour needs a
second cue (a mark, a word, a shape) for the people and the conditions in
which hue is not available.

A large field of one strong colour is a different act from a small accent,
and usually the more memorable one. There is no fixed proportion to follow;
the subject's own materials show how its colours are distributed.

## Imagery

Decide what the image is for before deciding what it shows: evidence (this is
the place, the object, the result), explanation (how it works, where it is),
or atmosphere. Evidence must be true to its subject and is cropped to show
it; explanation is drawn or annotated; atmosphere can be invented, provided
it is plainly illustration and not offered as a photograph of something real.

A set of images needs one treatment to read as a set: a shared viewpoint for
objects to be compared, a shared crop ratio and horizon for places, one
drawing manner for diagrams. Let the image's own structure place the type: a
headline belongs in the calm region of the picture, not in a box laid over
its subject. Real product views do more for a working tool's page than a
drawing of imaginary software.

## Motion

Sort motion by who started it. A response to an action should begin
immediately and finish fast, around 100 to 200 milliseconds for small changes
and up to about 400 for a large surface moving; it starts where the action
happened and can be interrupted or reversed without waiting. Movement the
page starts on its own is a claim on attention: one composed moment, tied to
the page's subject, lands where a sequence of unrelated entrances does not.

Operations repeated many times in a session lose their choreography first.
Changing data should be legible at once; an animated transition on every
refresh hides which value changed. Under `prefers-reduced-motion`, replace
movement with an instant change or a short fade; do not remove content that
motion was revealing, and make sure nothing is reachable only through an
animation. Movement that starts by itself, runs for more than five seconds
and sits beside other content needs a way to pause, stop or hide it.

## The familiar looks, in more detail

The looks listed in SKILL.md are those most often reported, in late 2026,
when language models design a page without a direction of their own. They
will date; whatever replaces them is recognizable by the same question,
whether an unrelated subject would have received the same thing.

- **Warm editorial.** Cream or off-white ground, a high-contrast serif for
  display with one phrase in italic, a terracotta, burgundy or forest accent,
  thin rules, small tracked capitals. Right when the subject's own printed
  matter, lettering or materials look like this, as a small press's or a
  bindery's do; as a default it signals taste without saying anything about
  the subject.
- **Dark technical.** Near-black ground, one acid green, orange or cyan
  accent, monospace labels, thin glowing borders. Use in low light justifies
  the dark ground and nothing more; the accent, the glow and the monospace
  each need a source in the product, such as a terminal it really lives in.
- **Gradient product.** White or deep navy, a blue-to-violet gradient, soft
  blurred shapes, translucent rounded cards. Right when a brand already owns
  it; being software is not that condition.
- **Broadsheet.** Hairline rules, dense columns, a masthead, small capitals,
  no radius anywhere. Right when readers scan many stories at once and a
  front page's hierarchy is the point; being published in issues is not
  enough.
- **Poster.** Heavy condensed or wood-type capitals set tight, sometimes
  running off the edge, on a flat saturated ground with one second colour.
  Right for a subject whose own printed matter looks like that, such as a
  dance or fight poster; given to any request for boldness, it arrives
  whatever the subject.
- **The card kit.** Every piece of content in a rounded card with the same
  radius and shadow, arranged in threes. Right for independent objects that
  people browse and pick up one at a time; when they are compared attribute
  by attribute, aligned rows serve better, and an argument cut into cards
  loses its order.

The faces that arrive with them change faster than the looks. At present they
include Inter and its close relatives (Manrope, DM Sans) for everything;
Fraunces, Playfair Display, Instrument Serif, Newsreader or Source Serif,
often with an italic line, for a literary page; Bricolage Grotesque, Archivo
or Barlow Condensed when a page should have character; IBM Plex Mono or Space
Mono for a technical label; Atkinson Hyperlegible for a friendly public page.
Each is a good face for some job; the question is the same as for the looks.

The same holds one level down, for copy: the headline made of two short
sentences with a full stop each, the three benefits with an icon apiece, the
closing section that repeats the opening. Each is a structure that fits any
subject, which is the reason to look twice before using it for this one.
