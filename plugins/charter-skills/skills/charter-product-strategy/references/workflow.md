# Worked Strategy Contrast: Two Ways Into Reconciliation

Consider a product that reconciles inconsistent transaction records before a
reporting deadline. The choices below are illustrative, not market findings,
price benchmarks or forecasts. Both can be viable; they need different buyers,
channels and delivery systems. Their contrast shows why "start with the
highest-paying customer" or "make it self-serve" cannot decide the wedge.

## Two coherent initial offers

| Choice | A: Small bookkeeping practices | B: Mid-market finance teams |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Switching occasion | Monthly close for clients already exporting the same supported records | A multi-entity close repeatedly delayed by reconciliation exceptions |
| User, buyer and payer | Bookkeeper uses it; practice owner buys and pays | Analysts use it; controller sponsors it; finance budget and procurement govern purchase |
| Current alternative | Manual spreadsheet matching inside the practice | Internal scripts plus analyst work and review coordination |
| Offer and advantage thesis | A repeatable exception workflow for a narrow file format; practitioner referrals may reach the next practice cheaply | A managed reconciliation outcome with accountable implementation; domain expertise may earn trust and uncover reusable rules |
| Channel | Practitioner partner introduces a paid monthly trial | Founder-led sale through a finance adviser, followed by a scoped paid implementation |
| Price and service | $150/month; two onboarding hours; bounded support | $18,000/year including sixty implementation hours and continuing analyst support |
| Exclusions | No bespoke connectors or managed close service | No unrelated finance transformation or unlimited data cleanup |

The lower price in A is sustained by repeatability and a concentrated channel,
not by calling the product software. B buys more accountability, not merely
more seats. Its service work can create reusable capability, but only if the
exceptions recur across customers and the business has rights to reuse what it
learns. Customer-specific cleanup can instead remain customer-specific labor.

## Make the economic units comparable

Assume a fully retained first twelve months from contract start, a loaded labor
cost of $60/hour, and the following illustrative costs. Contribution here means
revenue less variable delivery, support and onboarding; acquisition is shown
separately. Fixed engineering, overhead, tax and financing costs are excluded.
The same labor rate applies to founders: unpaid work still consumes capacity.

| First-year amount per customer | A | B |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue | $1,800 | $18,000 |
| Delivery infrastructure | $360 ($30/month) | $1,800 ($150/month) |
| Ongoing support | $360 (0.5 hours/month) | $2,880 (4 hours/month) |
| Onboarding / implementation | $120 (2 hours) | $3,600 (60 hours) |
| Contribution before acquisition | $960 | $9,720 |
| Acquisition cost, including selling labor | $240 | $2,400 |
| Contribution after acquisition | $720 | $7,320 |

B contributes much more per retained customer, but customer count is not the
scarce unit if the founder must sell, implement and support every account.
Implementation alone uses thirty times A's labor per customer. Conversely,
A's first acquisition cost does not automatically hold for the next ten
customers: partner reach, referral incentives and lead quality constrain it.

A's recurring monthly contribution is $90 before recovering onboarding and
acquisition. Its $360 initial burden takes four paid months to recover under
these assumptions. Losing the customer after two months leaves $180 unrecovered.
A twelve-month table is therefore a retention scenario, not lifetime value.

## Cash timing can overturn the margin ranking

Suppose A charges monthly in advance. It still finances acquisition and setup,
but receives cash soon after the sale. Suppose B invoices its annual fee after
a ninety-day implementation and the customer pays sixty days later. B earns
an attractive modeled contribution while financing roughly five months before
collection. For illustration, if its $6,000 acquisition and setup burden is
paid before collection and monthly delivery/support costs are $390, the
pre-collection cash requirement is about $7,950. The actual trough depends on
when costs are paid, not just when they are recognized.

For a team with $5,000 available for customer acquisition and delivery, no
financing, and access to the assumed practitioner channel, **choose A first**.
It supports smaller commitments and earlier receipts while testing a repeatable
workflow. B's margin does not pay obligations that fall due before its invoice.
This is a working-capital constraint on the initial choice, not a claim that B
is a worse eventual business. A long enterprise sales cycle would add time and
cost before even the modeled contract start.

Now change a few conditions rather than changing the product's feature list.
Suppose B's buyers accept a 50% payment at signing, before implementation costs
fall due; repeated implementations need twenty hours and support two hours per
month; and the assumed acquisition cost remains achievable. First-year
contribution after acquisition becomes:

```text
$18,000 - $1,800 - $1,440 - $1,200 - $2,400 = $11,160
```

The $9,000 deposit can cover the modeled initial burden, and implementation
capacity stretches further. **Choose B first under those conditions** if the
team can deliver the promised outcome. The strongest case against A is now
that it would consume scarce product and channel effort for much less
contribution while B creates reusable expertise and trust. The strongest case
against B remains that the deposit, repeatable labor reduction and sales access
may not survive actual buying and delivery. A deposit is not free cash: it
finances a delivery obligation and may carry refund terms.

Other reversals need no dramatic price change. If A needs two support hours
per customer per month, its $150 price is entirely consumed by $30 delivery
and $120 support before acquisition or onboarding. Its apparently scalable
offer then needs a narrower service promise, different economics or rejection.
If B's exceptions never repeat, its implementation reduction is imaginary and
the service-heavy offer must stand on its actual labor and cash requirements.

## Choose the next bet by the disputed link

For the initial A recommendation, a bounded paid cohort through the proposed
partner can deliver a real close outcome while revealing onboarding time,
support burden and channel conversion. Repeated paid use across close cycles
tests whether value survives novelty. It does not yet establish the partner's
ability to supply many more similar customers.

For B, a scoped paid implementation with explicit payment and acceptance terms
can test the working-capital premise and record actual delivery labor. A second
comparable implementation can distinguish reusable capability from heroic
first-customer service. Neither a signed proposal nor one successful delivery
establishes repeatable acquisition, renewal or rights to reuse customer data.

The next offer should narrow, expand or stop when the economically decisive
condition changes: service burden exhausts the price, a channel cannot supply
qualified buyers, or cash terms exceed available working capital. Pursuing
both wedges at once is not diversification if both depend on the same
implementation bottleneck.
