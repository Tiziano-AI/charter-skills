# Learning, pacing and play observation

Use this when players stall, repeat without improving, tire of the activity or
behave differently from what the design intended. The same pause or failure
can come from different causes, and lowering difficulty answers only some.

## Identify the learning problem

Follow what the player was trying to do and what they believed would happen.
Four nearby conditions often need different interventions:

| Condition | Useful intervention | Tempting intervention that can miss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The player does not know how to act. | Make the available input, target or affordance legible. | Reveal the puzzle solution when the missing fact is how to push. |
| The player acts but misunderstands a rule. | Give a clear, interpretable consequence or a contrasting example. | Repeat the control instructions more loudly. |
| The rule is understood but the solution is not yet found. | Preserve productive experimentation; add a graded hint if the intended experience calls for one. | Explain the deduction that was meant to be the play. |
| The intended action is understood but difficult to execute. | Examine input, spatial demands, access and the chosen skill challenge. | Rewrite the tutorial without changing the execution barrier. |

These conditions can overlap. A player may choose the correct action but
mistime it because the animation implies a different valid window. Watch the
attempt before labeling it a lack of skill or comprehension.

## Teaching through situations

A constrained early encounter can let the player discover a rule with little
competing information, then apply it in a changed arrangement. The changed
arrangement matters: repeating the same solution can train a sequence of
inputs without teaching the relation. Make the consequence visible enough to
connect action and result.

Layout, animation and sound are useful teachers when the rule can be exposed
through them. Text is useful for information that is hard to infer, unfamiliar
controls, access or a player's preferred way of learning. "Show, don't tell"
is not a ban on explanation. Nor must every game withhold complexity until a
tutorial grants it; an experienced audience may need direct access to the
whole system.

Separate teaching an action from revealing the answer. In a block puzzle, a
safe first push can establish that a block stops at a wall while leaving later
spatial reasoning intact. In a mystery, an explanation of every clue can
remove the pleasure of forming the theory. Provide what the intended play
requires, not every fact the designer knows.

## Progress changes what repetition means

Progression can add a capability, expose a new combination, change a
relationship or let the player recognize something previously opaque. A
larger health bar changes play if it permits a new risk or tactic; if enemies
scale in lockstep and nothing else changes, the number may only represent
advancement. That representation can still serve a desired fantasy, but it
does not by itself create new decisions.

Let a new element interact with established knowledge. A familiar action in a
new spatial arrangement can create depth without new controls. Conversely,
more complexity can burden players before they have a reason to care about
it. A deliberately open sandbox may give the whole palette early because
self-directed combination is the point.

Repetition can support mastery, comfort or ritual. It becomes a problem when
the player wants a changed possibility and the activity provides none. Do not
replace a calming familiar action with forced novelty merely to increase
event density.

## Rhythm gives events contrast

Intensity depends on what surrounds it. Constant danger can become tiring or
ordinary; an interval to explore, prepare or understand consequences can make
the next encounter matter. A pause can also lose direction if there is nothing
worth noticing or anticipating. Choose according to the intended rhythm rather
than treating every lull as abandonment.

Failure and recovery influence that rhythm. A retry can support learning when
it preserves an understandable relation between attempt and result. Repeating
an already-mastered approach before every difficult move may add friction
without useful practice. But removing the approach can remove suspense or a
resource decision the whole encounter depends on. Judge the complete attempt,
not just the instant of failure.

## Observe the question you actually have

For first-use comprehension, let someone encounter the game without explaining
the intended route. Once coached, that attempt can reveal execution or later
learning, but no longer reveals an unaided first encounter. Think-aloud can
expose beliefs while also interrupting timing and absorption; choose it for
the question, not as a universal playtest format.

Watch decisions and their context, then use questions to recover what the
player noticed or intended. "What were you trying to do there?" exposes a
different fact from "Was that fun?" A failed move may be an understood,
welcomed challenge; completion may come from brute force, assistance or a
misunderstood rule. Reported enjoyment matters but does not identify which
mechanism produced it.

Technical checks can establish inputs, transitions or persistence. Playing,
listening and observing can reveal control feel, masked audio, confusion and
rhythm that those checks omit. An agent's simulated play or critique can expose
a contradiction, but is not evidence that a human found the game compelling.

Choose the smallest observation that can distinguish the live explanations.
If a player understands the rule once the camera exposes the target, that
supports a visibility repair over a longer tutorial. If they still cannot
predict the consequence, the learning question remains. No fixed participant
count, metric sheet or check of every asset is required by this method.

## Sources and scope

[Stuart Burfield, *Reflections on tutorial design in Puzzledorf*](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/tutorial-design-in-puzzledorf-reflections)
and [*Reflections on playtesting and Puzzledorf*](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/reflections-on-playtesting-and-puzzledorf)
inform the distinction between understanding how to play and finding a
solution, teaching through arrangement, and observing without intervening.
They are the developer's accounts of a block-pushing puzzle, not evidence
that text-free tutorials, a particular test size or the same difficulty
curve fit every game. The pacing contrasts here are design hypotheses to
check against the intended play, not findings measured in those articles.
