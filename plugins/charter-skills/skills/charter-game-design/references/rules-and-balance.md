# Rules and balance

Use this when decisions are repetitive, resources seem pointless, randomness
obscures agency or one strategy appears to crowd out the rest. Balance is
relative to the contest or experience promised; it is not equal power for
every object in every situation.

## Alternatives need reasons to differ

Strategic depth comes from evaluating alternatives whose desirability depends
on state, future possibilities or another player's response. Adding attacks
that are all worse versions of the same attack adds selection effort without
adding that judgment. Different reach, commitment, exposure, positioning or
information can give an action its own useful situation.

Compare whole consequences rather than one stat. A strong move might trade
recovery, accuracy, range, resources or predictability for damage. A nominal
counter is not useful if it cannot be recognized or executed in the situation
where it is needed. Conversely, a move need not be equally useful everywhere:
a situational answer may earn its place by changing how an opponent plays.

Distinguish a dominant strategy from one that is popular, easy to learn or
strong against current opponents. A strategy is dominant in the relevant
model if alternatives cannot improve the outcome against the situations or
responses being considered. A usage rate alone does not establish that.
Players may choose a familiar option despite a better alternative, or choose
an expressive favorite for reasons outside win rate.

When a large option set feels shallow, first look for a decision where two
options would be attractive for different reasons. Change that relationship
before adding a new subsystem. In a story, however, two choices may earn their
place through voice or identity rather than different strategic outcomes.

## Scarcity creates strategy only when something desirable is forgone

A resource creates opportunity cost when spending it on one desirable use
precludes another. If ore buys only the next mandatory upgrade, raising its
price can lengthen collection without adding a strategic choice. Making
health, a shortcut and a situational tool compete for that ore changes the
decision, but only if each is useful in circumstances the player encounters.

Space, action opportunities, inventory slots and information can be resources
as well as currency. Two units sharing a narrow route compete for position;
moving a scout instead of firing gives up an opportunity. These constraints
often create more connected decisions than a new currency with its own shop.

Follow sources, storage, uses and replenishment far enough to see what players
can actually afford. If reserves make every purchase affordable, the apparent
trade-off disappears. If deprivation prevents any recovery, the player may
lose meaningful decisions rather than gain tension. A deliberately relaxing
collection game can value accumulation itself; scarcity is not a universal
repair for repetition.

## Information and uncertainty shape agency

Separate uncertainty before a decision from uncertainty in its resolution.
A changing hand asks the player to adapt to available options; a hit roll asks
them to tolerate or manage risk after choosing. Both can support play, but
they place agency in different places. Expected payoff alone omits the cost of
a bad tail outcome, the ability to recover and whether the player understands
the gamble.

Useful information arrives while it can affect a decision. An attack's
wind-up may permit a dodge; a damage number after impact explains a result but
cannot replace that warning. Hidden information can create bluffing and
deduction when players can infer possibilities and act on them. Revealing it
all can remove that play. Where surprise is intended, shape its consequence
and what can be learned afterward rather than assuming every surprise must be
preventable.

## Balance the appropriate unit

A position earned through earlier decisions can be locally overwhelming
without making the whole match unfair. Equalizing every encounter would erase
the payoff for good preparation. Inspect how the position is reached, what
responses remain and when the eventual result becomes inevitable.

Positive feedback lets an advantage buy further advantage; it can reward good
play but can also leave a long, decided ending. Counterplay, bounded resource
growth or a clearer ending address different causes. A comeback mechanism may
keep participation alive, but can make earlier success feel punished or
irrelevant. Decide which experience the game promises before choosing one.

Symmetric starts are not required for a fair contest; asymmetric roles can
balance across objectives, encounters or a match. Nor is competitive fairness
the target of every game. A powerful story ability may deliver a fantasy; the
question is whether it destroys another part of that experience, not whether
every ability has the same efficiency.

## A useful contrast

In a tactics game, three attacks costing the same action and differing only
in increasing damage leave one obvious choice. Adding a fourth repeats the
defect. Let a heavy attack expose the attacker, while a lighter one preserves
position, if anticipating retaliation is the intended play. Then observe
whether players change their choice with the board state, rather than merely
rotate through the buttons.

In a dialogue scene, three responses can leave the plot unchanged while
offering different ways to inhabit a character. Giving every reply a tactical
bonus could turn expression into optimization. The relevant promise decides
whether identical outcomes are a problem.

## Sources and scope

[David Sirlin, *Balancing Multiplayer Games, Part 2: Viable Options*](https://www.sirlin.net/articles/balancing-multiplayer-games-part-2-viable-options)
informs the distinction between viable options and local advantage in
competitive play. It does not require making every option equally useful or
making every game competitive.

[JB Oger, *No Scarcity, No Strategy*](https://jboger.substack.com/p/no-scarcity-no-strategy)
uses development of *Drop Duchy* to explain competing uses and opportunity
cost. That design account supports a strategic lens, not scarcity as a
condition of all worthwhile play. The contrasts above apply these ideas to
design choices; they are not measured player outcomes.
