# Discovery, expression and social play

Use this when shaping exploration, interpretation, narrative choice or
participation with others, including in games built around combat, rewards or
persistent consequences. If the proposed repair adds a contest, reward, failure
state or lasting consequence, ask what it does to the intended experience:
those additions can help a game that wants them and change the point of one
that does not.

## Curiosity needs something to pursue

Exploration becomes meaningful when a perceptible detail raises a question
and pursuing it changes understanding. A room with many interchangeable props
can be less inviting than a room with one suggestive inconsistency. Connect
placement, traces of events, sound and access so that looking more closely or
moving to another viewpoint reveals a relation, not just another collectible.

Distinguish a lack of hooks from deliberate quiet. If a player cannot form a
question or imagine a next action, clearer spatial invitation may help. If
they are connecting clues at their own pace, a quest marker that supplies the
answer can remove the activity. Rewards can also change attention: a numeric
completion meter encourages exhausting a list, which may displace wondering
what happened in a place.

An authored world can be worth discovering without being a simulation that
lets the player rewrite it. Arrange the material so possible paths reveal
coherent information, while leaving room for missed details and different
orders when those serve the experience. Repetition earns its place through a
changed context or reading, not simply by making the player revisit a node.

## Interpretation and actionable information

Environmental storytelling works through associations among elements. A
displaced chair, an unfinished meal and an open window can suggest a chain of
events without an explanatory caption. Their relation matters more than the
quantity of decoration. Draw them from the world's premise so that the player
has a context in which to interpret them.

An interpretation need not reproduce the author's private account to be
valuable. If the scene conveys a troubled relationship, competing readings of
the details may be intended. If that same detail is the only signal of a
lethal hazard, its legibility has a different job. Decide whether ambiguity
supports interpretation, deduction or surprise, or merely hides information
needed for the promised kind of action.

Simulation can support or damage an authored scene: physics that casually
moves a crucial prop can erase its connection to an event. Conversely, traces
left by the player's own actions can become meaningful history. Preserve the
relationship the scene depends on rather than choosing maximum simulation or
maximum scripting in the abstract.

## Narrative choices have different jobs

Choose a structure for what the player should be able to understand and
influence. Ashwell's patterns offer useful contrasts, not exclusive genres:

- A heavily branching tree supports possibility and replay but leaves much
  material unseen and can make the whole space hard to grasp.
- A largely linear path with short, rejoining branches can sustain reflective
  or rhetorical choices while retaining an authored sequence.
- Branches that repeatedly rejoin can still accumulate consequences through
  state. When the promise is that earlier decisions matter later, preserve
  their effects across the join instead of silently resetting them.
- A reversible map gives the player room to understand a place; it also
  reduces forward pressure compared with a directed journey.
- Returning through a familiar structure can reveal changed options or
  meaning, provided repetition has an intelligible role in the fiction.

The quality question is what the choice promises. Selecting how to say goodbye
can be expressive even when the train departs in every branch. Offering a
choice of whom to save and then forgetting that choice breaks a different
promise. Additional endings will not automatically repair weak expression;
immediate consequences will not automatically improve a story built around
delayed recognition.

## Rules shape participation, not only outcomes

In shared play, establish what participants are trying to make together and
what the rules protect. A competitive game may need comparable chances and
counterplay. A roleplaying game may enable characters to hurt or oppose each
other while protecting agreement among the people playing them. Another may
explore giving, receiving and drawing closer or apart without treating every
relationship as latent conflict.

Mechanisms distribute who can introduce information, make decisions, act and
respond. Asymmetric knowledge can create cooperation, but may also leave one
person issuing instructions to everyone else. Shared goals alone do not
guarantee meaningful participation. Give other players information, situated
judgment or expressive authority that matters to the intended collaboration;
do not add secrecy or rigid turn limits when the point is open co-creation.

Distinguish quiet participation from exclusion. Listening can be an intended
contribution; having no way to affect anything despite wanting to is another
condition. Likewise, a fictional setback can create a welcome dramatic
opportunity. Removing all losses can flatten that play, while mechanically
forcing another participant's expression can undermine the agreement that
makes it possible.

## A useful contrast

In a shared tale, each player can decide what their character offers another
and the recipient can decide how to receive it. A scoring rule for the
"best" offer can turn a relationship scene into a performance contest. If
the brief instead calls for a competitive negotiation game, incentives,
credible refusals and comparable opportunities may be essential. The same
surface action does not identify the design problem.

## Sources and scope

[Sam Kabo Ashwell, *Standard Patterns in Choice-Based Games*](https://heterogenoustasks.wordpress.com/2015/01/26/standard-patterns-in-choice-based-games/)
provides the structural contrasts and their trade-offs. It is a non-exhaustive
analysis of works, not a measured ranking of narrative structures.

[Vincent Baker, *Powered by the Apocalypse, part 10: 3 Underlying Models*](https://lumpley.games/2023/12/17/powered-by-the-apocalypse-part-10-3-underlying-models/)
contrasts character conflict with player agreement, relationships, and
adventure. These are examples of different design foundations, not a complete
taxonomy of social play.

[Matthias Worch and Harvey Smith, *What Happened Here? Environmental Storytelling*](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc10/slides/Smith_Harvey_WhatHappenedHereWeb_Notes.pdf)
distinguishes interpretation, telegraphing, authored staging and systemic
traces. Its theoretical explanations are design arguments; this reference
does not treat the talk as empirical proof of player investment.
